## Summary

A small wind rotor whose blades are articulated: they can fold toward the hub and change pitch, so the rotor presents a different solidity and a different blade geometry as the wind changes. The experiment optimises the blade shape and the fold/pitch schedule for annual energy at a stated site, subject to a thrust limit and a cut-out condition — and then checks the cheap model it optimised with (blade-element momentum theory) against a moving-mesh CFD solution for two configurations, because a BEM-only result would be a design without a verification.

## Question

How much annual energy does an articulated rotor gain or lose compared with the best fixed-geometry rotor of the same swept area and the same generator, and what does the fold buy in peak thrust at the same energy?

## Why run it

- It is a clean multi-objective problem with a physical trade (energy against loads) where the answer is not obvious in advance: folding reduces area and therefore both power and loads, so the optimum may be a fixed rotor that never folds.
- Wind energy is an industrial market where a verified surrogate-plus-CFD workflow has immediate value, unlike most of the speculative series.
- It generates a defensible coefficient-versus-tip-speed-ratio surface, which is the artefact every small-turbine design needs and few have.

## Physical model

**Aerodynamics.** Blade-element momentum theory with Prandtl's tip and hub loss factors for the cheap model: induction factors solved iteratively along the blade, lift and drag from a polar, thrust and torque integrated over the span. The validity range — axisymmetric inflow, moderate induction, no yaw, no dynamic stall — is stated, and yaw and dynamic inflow are excluded rather than approximated.

**Blade polar.** Either a published low-Reynolds polar for the chosen section family, or a 2-D polar computed with a panel method and corrected with a stated stall-delay model; the choice and its provenance are recorded per polar point.

**Loads.** Thrust from BEM, plus a structural check of the blade root for the worst load case, using a beam idealisation (`beam_fe`).

**Fold kinematics.** The fold angle changes the effective span and the local pitch; the parameterisation is (fold angle, pitch) as a function of wind speed, with an actuation rate limit and a hinge loss model.

**Deliberately out of scope:** yaw dynamics, tower shadow, unsteady aerodynamics, aeroelasticity, and a full rotor–nacelle–tower flow solution.

## Geometry and inputs

Blade planform (chord and twist distributions), section family, and the fold hinge location are the design variables; the fold/pitch schedule is a second, discrete decision per wind bin. Geometry generation uses `pinneapple_design.geometry.gen.airfoil` for sections and `design.geometry.mesh` for meshing; the rotor is assembled parametrically.

Inputs: a site wind-speed distribution (a Weibull fit with stated A and k), air density, generator efficiency curve, and the thrust and cut-out limits.

## Solver and engine

- Cheap model: a BEM implementation (to be written; see the work items) with the polar from either a panel method (`pinneapple_design.aero.panel`) or published data.
- Verification: `external_solvers.openfoam` with a moving-mesh (AMI) rotor case for two configurations — the fixed baseline and the optimum at the fold angle where the schedule is most used.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A BEM solver** with induction iteration, tip/hub losses, a polar interface and a per-wind-bin evaluation — proposed `pinneapple_simulation/numerical_solvers/bem.py`.
2. **The fold kinematics and its aerodynamic effect** on the model: effective span, local pitch offset and the hinge loss, with a validity note where the folded blade violates the BEM assumptions.
3. **A moving-mesh rotor case template** (AMI or a sliding interface) at two blade counts, for the verification runs.
4. **An annual-energy model** that integrates the power curve over the site distribution, with the fold schedule as a decision per bin and a stated hysteresis rule for actuator wear.
5. **An actuator duty model**: number of fold cycles per year and the energy they consume, so the schedule cannot be free.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the BEM against published rotor data (a known rotor with a published power curve and geometry) before optimising anything; report the discrepancy as a function of tip-speed ratio.
2. Establish the fixed-rotor baseline: best planform for the same swept area and generator, optimised with the same budget.
3. Optimise the articulated rotor (planform plus schedule) for annual energy with the thrust constraint; record the Pareto front of energy against peak thrust.
4. Run the two CFD verification cases and compare C_p and thrust against BEM; report the disagreement and whether it changes the ranking.
5. Recompute the optimum with the actuator duty model and report how much energy the articulation costs over a year.
6. Compute budget: minutes per BEM evaluation, so the optimum is cheap; the CFD verification cases are the expensive part — order 10^2 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Fixed-geometry rotor of the same swept area and generator, optimised under the same budget. This is the baseline that decides the issue.
- Published rotor data for the BEM validation (geometry and power curve both published; if only one is available, the issue says so and validates only what it has).
- CFD at two configurations, with GCI on one of them, against the BEM prediction.
- Structural check: root bending moment against the blade material limit with a stated safety factor.

## Metrics

- C_p versus tip-speed ratio, dimensionless, for every fold state.
- Annual energy production, kWh/year, integrated over the site distribution.
- Peak thrust, kN, and its reduction relative to the fixed rotor.
- Root bending moment, N·m, at the worst case.
- Actuator duty: fold cycles per year and their energy, kWh/year.
- BEM–CFD disagreement: relative error in C_p and thrust at the verification points.

## Deliverables

- The BEM implementation with its validation report.
- The C_p–TSR surface for the fold states, as data and figures.
- The optimised planform and schedule, the Pareto front, and the fixed-rotor comparison.
- The two CFD verification cases with their convergence data.
- A short animation of the fold schedule over a year of wind bins, from the campaign video pipeline.

## Acceptance criteria

- BEM reproduces the published rotor within a stated band (for example 10 % in C_p at the design tip-speed ratio) before optimisation; if it does not, the gap is diagnosed rather than tuned away.
- CFD agrees with BEM within the stated band at the two verification points, or the disagreement is reported and the ranking re-examined.
- The articulated rotor's annual energy advantage over the fixed baseline exceeds the actuator duty and the model uncertainty, or the report concludes that articulation does not pay.
- Every reported configuration satisfies the thrust limit and the cut-out rule.
- The root bending moment stays below the material limit with the stated safety factor.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for small wind rotors and blade articulation (both exist commercially at small scale); `extrapolated` for this specific schedule optimisation. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (BEM residual and the momentum balance), `numerical_stability` (CFD GCI, polar resolution), `physical_plausibility` (published rotor validation, Betz limit as a bound), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (site distribution and polar uncertainty propagated to annual energy). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed without measurements; `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the actuator duty model but not by a mechanism design.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **BEM is used outside its range at high fold angles.** Mitigation: a stated validity envelope, and CFD verification exactly at the extreme fold state.
2. **The polars are unrepresentative** at the low Reynolds numbers of a small rotor. Mitigation: report polar provenance and run the optimisation with two polar sets, showing the sensitivity.
3. **A generous site distribution manufactures the gain.** Mitigation: fix the site distribution as a design input before optimisation, and report the result for two contrasting sites.
4. **The optimum sits on the constraint boundary** and is fragile. Mitigation: report the distance to the thrust and cut-out limits for the optimum.
5. **Actuator wear ignored.** Mitigation: duty cycles counted and their energy subtracted.

## Out of scope

- Generator and power-electronics design, MPPT strategy.
- Yaw control, tower and foundation design.
- Certification loads and a full aeroelastic analysis.
