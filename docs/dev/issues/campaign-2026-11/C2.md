## Summary

A cloud of superparamagnetic particles under a time-varying magnetic field aggregates into fingers, a palm and an opposing thumb, closes on a sphere, and then reconfigures into a three-finger tool. The experiment computes the field sequence that produces each shape without letting the particles collapse into an uncontrolled clump, and it reports the grip force the assembly can actually transmit — which is the number that decides whether the demonstration means anything.

## Question

For a stated particle size, magnetisation and fluid viscosity, what is the largest grip force an aggregated particle gripper can hold against a load, and which field sequence maximises it while keeping the aggregation stable?

## Why run it

- The physics that decides the answer is not the picture: dipole–dipole attraction grows with the square of the magnetisation and the inverse fourth power of separation, while viscous drag at low Reynolds number is linear in size and velocity — so a small parameter change moves the design between a usable gripper and a clump.
- Magnetic manipulation of particles is used in practice (seals, dampers, micro-manipulation), so the result is transferable, unlike a free-floating nanorobot claim.
- It produces the failure map: the field at which the shape loses its fingers and collapses.

## Physical model

**Particles.** Superparamagnetic spheres with induced dipole moment `m = (4πa³/3)·(3χ/(χ+3))·H` under field `H`, interacting through the dipole–dipole potential, with excluded volume and a steric short-range repulsion standing in for a surfactant layer.

**Field.** `H(r, t)` from a set of coils, computed with `pinneapple_simulation.electromagnetics.statics` and `.analytic`; the field gradient exerts the body force `∇(m·B)` that moves a particle centre.

**Fluid.** Low-Reynolds-number drag in the Stokes regime (`F = 6πμa·u` relative to the local fluid velocity); Brownian motion is included where the Péclet number puts it in play.

**Aggregation limit.** The dipole–dipole attraction must be balanced against thermal energy at the intended scale; the ratio sets whether an assembly persists once the field is removed, which is exactly the difference between a gripper and a dust cloud.

**Deliberately out of scope:** particle–particle magnetic saturation effects inside a chain, magnetohydrodynamic flow, and any chemistry of binding — the assembly is held by the field, not by bonds.

## Geometry and inputs

Particles: diameter in the 1–100 µm range (a design choice, stated up front), log-normal size distribution, volume fraction, and magnetisation. Field geometry: 4–8 coils around the working volume, optimised for the sequence. Target shapes: a two-finger pinch and a three-finger tool around a rigid sphere of stated radius.

## Solver and engine

- Particle dynamics: `pinneapple_simulation.particle_dynamics.particles` and `.rigid_body` for the units, with the dipole interaction added (see the work items); `numerical_solvers.neighbors` for the neighbour search.
- Field: `pinneapple_simulation.electromagnetics.statics`, `.analytic`, `.media`/`.materials` for the susceptibility.
- Field sequence optimisation: `pinneapple_design.design_optimizer` or a CMA-ES-style search over coil currents; `pinneapple_autolab` if the campaign should choose its own cases.
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A dipole–dipole interaction module** with the induced-moment law, excluded volume and a steric shell, differentiable where possible so the field sequence can be optimised through the simulation.
2. **A field-from-coils module**: currents to a field grid, with the gradient computed consistently (finite differences on the same grid, or analytically) — a wrong gradient makes the whole experiment meaningless.
3. **A grip-force estimator**: the force the assembly transmits to the held object, computed from the contact forces and the field, plus the maximum before the aggregate tears.
4. **A shape objective**: a distance between the particle configuration and the target shape that is stable under optimisation (a Chamfer or Hausdorff-type distance on the point set).
5. **A stability analysis**: the field strength at which each assembled shape loses its fingers, as a function of particle size and volume fraction.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the dipole interaction against the two-particle analytic force and against the chain-formation threshold; report the relative error.
2. Calibrate the drag and Brownian terms against the diffusion coefficient of an isolated particle (`D = kT/(6πμa)`).
3. Assemble each target shape with the optimised field sequence and record the shape error, the assembly time, and the field energy.
4. Measure the grip force: apply a ramped load to the held sphere and record the force at which the aggregate fails; repeat over particle size and volume fraction.
5. Map the collapse threshold: sweep field strength at fixed volume fraction and report where the shape transitions from fingers to clump.
6. Compute budget: the particle count is limited by the neighbour search — of order 10^4–10^5 particles for minutes per run; the sequence optimisation needs hundreds of runs, so budget 10^2–10^3 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Two-particle analytic dipole force and the known chain-formation criterion as the interaction reference.
- Diffusion coefficient of a single particle in the Stokes regime.
- A rigid gripper of the same closing span, as a force-per-unit-mass reference — the aggregate will lose, and by how much is the result.
- Energy bound: the magnetic field energy spent to hold a load must be reported beside the load, so the device cannot appear free.

## Metrics

- Shape error to the target, mm (or in particle diameters), and the fraction of particles in the intended fingers.
- Grip force, mN, and grip force per unit held mass, dimensionless against the rigid-gripper reference.
- Assembly time, s, and field energy, mJ, per assembly.
- Collapse field, mT, per shape and particle size.
- Aggregation persistence after field removal: fraction of the assembly retained after a stated time.

## Deliverables

- The dipole module, the coil-field module and the grip-force estimator, with tests.
- The shape and collapse maps as data and figures.
- The field sequences, with the coil currents and the resulting field energy.
- A render of one assembly and its grip test, with the field magnitude shown, from the campaign video pipeline.

## Acceptance criteria

- The two-particle force matches the analytic value within the stated tolerance, and the gradient used for the body force is verified against a finite-difference check.
- Each target shape is reached with a shape error below the stated threshold, or the report states which shapes are not reachable and why.
- The grip force is reported with its uncertainty from the particle size distribution, and the aggregate's failure mode is identified (finger tear, clump slip, chain buckling).
- The collapse field is reported per configuration; no configuration is presented as stable without it.
- The scale argument is explicit: what force a stated particle size and field can transmit, and why this does not support a hand-scale, human-strength claim.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `extrapolated`. Magnetic particle aggregation and magnetic micro-manipulation are established; a free-standing gripper of this capability is not demonstrated. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (analytic two-particle force, gradient check), `numerical_stability` (particle count and time-step convergence), `physical_plausibility` (Brownian/dipole balance, chain-formation criterion), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (size distribution and susceptibility spread). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed without a bench; `technological_feasibility` stays not assessed, though the grip-force and field-energy numbers bound it.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The gradient is inconsistent with the field**, producing plausible-looking motion for the wrong reason. Mitigation: the finite-difference gradient check is an acceptance criterion.
2. **The simulation scale is chosen to make the result work.** Mitigation: the particle size and volume fraction are declared design inputs, and the force scaling is reported across the range rather than at one point.
3. **A shape metric that rewards a pretty cloud** without the fingers that do the gripping. Mitigation: the fraction of particles in the intended contact region is reported beside the global shape error.
4. **Ignoring thermal motion** at small particle sizes makes an unstable assembly look stable. Mitigation: the Péclet number is reported and Brownian motion is included where it matters.
5. **The grip test is not the load path of the demonstration.** Mitigation: the load is applied along the same direction the demonstration uses.

## Out of scope

- Binding chemistry, surfactants and particle synthesis.
- Coil and amplifier hardware design beyond a current and power budget.
- In-vivo or biological application.
