## Summary

Helical blades and a shaft assemble from suspended particles in a viscous fluid, and then the assembly rotates under an applied torque and drives a flow. The experiment reports volumetric flow per unit torque, the stability of the assembly while it turns, and the Reynolds number at which the result stops being transferable to anything larger — because at low Reynolds number a rotor that works is a different machine from one that works in air.

## Question

Can a particle assembly held together by external fields form a rotor that pumps fluid at low Reynolds number, and what fraction of the applied torque appears as useful flow work once the assembly's own losses are accounted for?

## Why run it

- It joins two capabilities that are usually studied apart — self-assembly and hydrodynamic performance — and the join is where the real constraint lives: an assembly that pumps tends to be an assembly that comes apart.
- The low-Reynolds regime is where a rotating helix is a legitimate propulsion and pumping mechanism (the bacterial flagellum is the existence proof), so the result has a physical anchor rather than a science-fiction premise.
- It produces a scaling table: torque, flow and stability versus size, which says what the concept could do at a larger scale and where the physics changes.

## Physical model

**Hydrodynamics.** Stokes flow at low Reynolds number; the resistance of a rotating helix described by resistive-force theory (local drag coefficients for motion along and normal to the filament), with the full flow solved by an immersed-boundary or particle method at low resolution to check the local theory.

**Assembly.** Particles bound by short-range attraction into a helix: the bond must carry the torque, so the binding strength sets the maximum torque before the rotor slips or disassembles — this is the coupling that makes the experiment interesting.

**Rotation.** An external torque (magnetic, via a rotating field on magnetised particles, or optical) drives the shaft; the fluid response is the pump curve.

**Dimensionless groups.** Reynolds number based on helix radius and rotation rate (order 10^-3 to 10^-1), the helix pitch-to-radius ratio, and a dimensionless binding energy (bond strength over thermal energy).

**Deliberately out of scope:** inertia, turbulence, cavitation, and any biological function. The fluid is Newtonian and the particles are rigid.

## Geometry and inputs

A helix parametrised by radius, pitch, filament radius and number of turns; a shaft made of particles; the assembly is built from a stated particle size and bond model. The container is a channel of stated cross-section, so the flow can be measured against a pressure difference.

## Solver and engine

- Flow: `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.lbm` and `.lbm_tunnel` for the low-Reynolds channel flow, `particles`/`isph`/`dfsph` for a particle-based alternative, and `external_solvers.openfoam` for a body-fitted check on the assembled geometry.
- Assembly: `pinneapple_simulation.particle_dynamics.particles` and `.rigid_body` with the bond model added; `numerical_solvers.neighbors` for contact detection.
- Torque and work accounting: computed from the solver fields on the rotor surface and on the channel sections.
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A bonded-assembly model**: particles joined into a helix with bonds that carry torque and can fail, including the failure criterion.
2. **Two-way coupling at low Reynolds number**: the rotating assembly in the flow, stable enough to run for many revolutions without the coupling drifting.
3. **Resistive-force theory as a reference implementation**, so the simulation can be compared against a closed-form estimate of thrust and torque for a helix.
4. **The slip criterion**: the torque at which the assembly deforms or disassembles, as a function of bond strength.
5. **A scaling table generator**: torque, flow, efficiency and stability across particle size and helix pitch, with the Reynolds number reported per entry.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the flow solver on a rotating cylinder in a channel against the analytic low-Re solution and against published drag values; report the error.
2. Validate resistive-force theory against the simulation for a rigid helix over a range of rotation rates; report the disagreement as a function of Reynolds number.
3. Run the assembly cases: build the helix, spin it up, measure the pump curve (pressure rise versus flow rate) and the torque.
4. Sweep bond strength to find the slip threshold at each rotation rate; report the operating envelope.
5. Compute budget: low-Re flow is cheap per step but needs many steps for a revolution — order 10^2 CPU-hours for the sweep.

## Baselines, references and verification

- A rigid, manufactured helix of the same geometry, spun at the same rate: the assembly's performance is a fraction of it, and the fraction is the headline.
- Resistive-force theory as the analytic reference at low Reynolds number.
- A rotating cylinder in a channel for the flow-solver validation.
- Torque and work balance: applied torque times rotation rate must equal the viscous dissipation plus the flow work within the stated tolerance.

## Metrics

- Flow rate, µL/min, and pressure rise, Pa, at a stated rotation rate (the pump curve).
- Volumetric flow per unit torque, µL·min⁻¹·(nN·m)⁻¹.
- Efficiency: flow work over applied work, dimensionless.
- Slip threshold: the torque at which the assembly fails, nN·m, per bond strength.
- Reynolds number per case, dimensionless, and the deviation from the rigid-helix baseline.
- Rotation lifetime before failure: revolutions.

## Deliverables

- The bonded-assembly model and the two-way coupling, with tests.
- The validation cases (rotating cylinder, rigid helix) and their error reports.
- The pump curves and the stability envelope as data and figures.
- One render of the assembled rotor pumping, with vorticity, from the campaign video pipeline.
- The scaling table with the Reynolds number attached to every row.

## Acceptance criteria

- The flow solver reproduces the rotating-cylinder reference within the stated tolerance.
- Resistive-force theory and simulation agree within a stated band at low rotation rates, and the deviation is reported where they diverge.
- The work balance closes within the stated tolerance for every accepted case.
- The slip threshold is reported for every configuration; no performance number is presented without the torque at which the assembly fails.
- The report states the Reynolds number, and no result is claimed to transfer to a macroscale rotor.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `hypothetical` for a self-assembled pumping rotor; `established` for helical pumping at low Reynolds number. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (work balance, resistive-force comparison), `numerical_stability` (time-step and resolution convergence over many revolutions), `physical_plausibility` (rotating-cylinder reference, low-Re regime), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (bond strength and particle size spread). `experimental_validation` and `technological_feasibility` stay not assessed; the issue states that no such rotor has been demonstrated.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The assembly is held by a field that also does the pumping**, so the reported efficiency counts work the field did elsewhere. Mitigation: separate the field that holds from the torque that drives, and report both energies.
2. **The coupling drifts** over the many revolutions a pump curve needs. Mitigation: independent momentum balance per revolution, and a stability check on the coupling scheme.
3. **Resistive-force theory is used outside its range** (thick filaments, close walls). Mitigation: state the slenderness and wall-distance limits, and report the deviation curve.
4. **The scale is chosen to make a nice number.** Mitigation: the scaling table spans a range and no single size is presented as the design.
5. **A visual of spinning particles mistaken for a pump.** Mitigation: the pump curve, with the pressure rise, is the result; the render is the illustration.

## Out of scope

- Particle synthesis, surface functionalisation and bonding chemistry.
- Magnetic drive hardware beyond a torque specification.
- Biological or medical application.
