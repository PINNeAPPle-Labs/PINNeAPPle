## Summary

A flat plate with programmed hinge lines folds itself into a three-dimensional shape when a stimulus is applied, and the design variable is the layout of hinges and the actuation field. The experiment asks how accurately a target shape can be reached, and — the part that is usually missing — whether the folded state is stable, whether the sheet self-intersects on the way, and whether the same physics can be demonstrated at bench scale before any nanoscale claim is made.

## Question

For a target three-dimensional shape and a flat sheet of stated size, what hinge layout and actuation field minimise the shape error at equilibrium, subject to foldability (no self-intersection, reachable rotations) and to the folded state being stable?

## Why run it

- It is the best entry point of the programmable-matter series because the physics is testable at bench scale: plate kinematics with elastic hinges is the same whether the sheet is millimetres or microns thick, so a validated model does not depend on a nanoscale claim.
- The shape-error objective makes it a genuine inverse problem rather than a fold-the-pretty-pattern exercise.
- Stability and self-intersection are where most origami designs fail, and reporting them is the difference between a design and a demonstration.

## Physical model

**Kinematics.** A plate discretised into facets joined by hinge lines; each hinge has a rest angle and a stiffness. Facets are treated as rigid in the first model and as elastic plates in the second, which is the model progression this issue proposes.

**Actuation.** Stimulus produces a target hinge angle either through an eigenstrain (a swelling or thermal expansion mismatch across the thickness) or a magnetic/thermal response; the actuation field defines the target angles.

**Energy.** Total elastic energy is the sum of hinge energies plus plate bending; equilibrium is the minimiser of that energy subject to the kinematic constraints of the crease pattern. Stability is the positive-definiteness of the Hessian at the minimum.

**Deliberately out of scope:** plasticity, viscoelasticity and rate effects, and the chemistry of any specific responsive polymer.

## Geometry and inputs

Flat sheet dimensions and thickness, a crease pattern (the design variable: which lines, which angles), a target mesh for each desired shape, and the actuation field. Shapes targeted in the first campaign: a capsule, a box, and a three-dimensional curved shell.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A crease-pattern model**: facets, hinge lines, rest angles and constraints, assembled into an element system the existing solvers can evaluate.
2. **A large-rotation plate element or a rigid-facet kinematic model**, since the existing beam and plane-stress elements do not handle the fold angles involved; the honest option is the rigid-facet model first, with the elastic correction as a second stage.
3. **A foldability check**: hinge angles within physical limits and no facet self-intersection along the folding path, implemented as a hard constraint.
4. **A shape-error objective**: a symmetric distance between the folded surface and the target mesh, computed on the same discretisation used by `error_map`.
5. **A stability test**: the Hessian spectrum at equilibrium, so a folded state that is a saddle is reported as such.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the rigid-facet model against a known fold pattern with a closed-form solution (a Miura-ori cell's kinematics) before any optimisation.
2. For each target shape, optimise the crease pattern and the actuation field for shape error under the foldability constraint; run at least three random starts per target.
3. Check stability of every reported equilibrium and report the lowest eigenvalue.
4. Re-evaluate the best designs with the elastic plate model and report how much the rigid-facet assumption changed the shape error.
5. Compute budget: minutes per equilibrium solve; hundreds of solves per target — order 10^1–10^2 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Closed-form kinematics of a Miura-ori or waterbomb cell as the model validation.
- Initial hand-designed pattern per target as the baseline the optimiser must beat.
- Rigid-facet versus elastic-plate comparison as the model-fidelity check.
- Self-intersection reported as a binary per design, verified by an independent intersection test, not by the optimiser's own penalty.

## Metrics

- Shape error: mean and 95th percentile distance from the folded surface to the target, in millimetres or per cent of sheet size.
- Foldability: boolean, plus the number of violated hinge limits.
- Stability: lowest eigenvalue of the energy Hessian at equilibrium, and the number of negative modes.
- Actuation magnitude required, in the units of the chosen stimulus, and its uniformity requirement.
- Model-fidelity change: shape error under the elastic model minus under the rigid model.

## Deliverables

- The crease-pattern model, the foldability check and the stability test.
- Optimised patterns for the three targets, with renders of the folded states and the target overlaid.
- The validation against closed-form kinematics and the rigid-versus-elastic comparison.
- A folding animation from the campaign video pipeline, with the hinge angles plotted against time.

## Acceptance criteria

- The rigid-facet model reproduces the closed-form cell kinematics within the stated tolerance.
- Every reported design is foldable without self-intersection by the independent test, and stable with all Hessian eigenvalues positive.
- The optimised pattern beats the hand-designed baseline on shape error by a margin larger than the model-fidelity change.
- The actuation magnitude is reported, and no design relies on a stimulus that cannot be applied to a real sheet.
- The bench-scale analogue is stated: which dimensions and materials would reproduce the same dimensionless behaviour.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for self-folding sheets at bench scale, `extrapolated` for the same at micron scale. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (closed-form kinematics, stability spectrum), `numerical_stability` (mesh and start-point sensitivity), `physical_plausibility` (foldability, energy at equilibrium), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (hinge stiffness spread). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed without a bench build; `technological_feasibility` stays not assessed, though the actuation magnitude and the bench analogue bound it.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The rigid-facet model hides a fold that elastic plates cannot perform.** Mitigation: the elastic re-evaluation is mandatory, and its delta is a reported metric.
2. **The optimiser cheats with a self-intersecting pattern.** Mitigation: independent intersection test as an acceptance criterion.
3. **A stable minimum reported from a Hessian computed on a coarse discretisation.** Mitigation: spectrum computed at two resolutions.
4. **Actuation that no material provides.** Mitigation: the actuation magnitude is a constraint with a stated limit, taken from a real responsive material.
5. **Micron-scale claims from a millimetre-scale model.** Mitigation: the report states the dimensionless conditions of the bench analogue and refuses the nanoscale claim.

## Out of scope

- Material synthesis and the chemistry of responsive polymers.
- Manufacturing of the crease pattern at scale.
- Multi-physics actuation (light, humidity, chemistry) beyond a magnitude and uniformity specification.
