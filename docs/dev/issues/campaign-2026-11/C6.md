## Summary

A ferrofluid surface under a variable magnetic field: it forms spikes, rings and other three-dimensional patterns, and the experiment asks which field produces a target shape, at what stability margin, and — first — whether the simulation reproduces the classical linear-stability result the phenomenon is known for. The last point is what separates this issue from a pretty fluid demo: the Rosensweig instability has a known critical magnetisation and a known most-unstable wavelength, and nothing else in the issue is credible until both are reproduced.

## Question

Given a target three-dimensional surface profile, can the applied field be optimised to approach it, and what is the range of shapes a field can command before the interface becomes unstable or the required field exceeds the fluid's magnetisation?

## Why run it

- Ferrofluids are real, used in seals, dampers and loudspeakers, so the result transfers.
- The instability is analytically tractable, which gives the experiment an exact reference — rare in this series.
- The output is a controllability map: which shapes are reachable and which are not, which is the honest answer to "can it become anything".

## Physical model

**Governing equations.** Incompressible Navier–Stokes with a magnetic body force, plus the normal-stress balance at the free surface including surface tension and the magnetic pressure jump.

**Magnetisation.** A nonlinear law `M(H)` (Langevin-type) with the susceptibility and saturation magnetisation as stated material data; linearisation around the operating point for the stability analysis.

**Stability reference.** The Rosensweig criterion gives the critical magnetisation for the onset of the spike instability and the wavelength of the fastest-growing mode, both as functions of surface tension, density and layer depth. These are the numbers the simulation must hit.

**Dimensionless groups.** Magnetic Bond number (magnetic stress over capillary stress), the layer depth over capillary length, and the viscosity ratio setting the growth rate.

**Deliberately out of scope:** field-dependent viscosity, particle agglomeration and settling over long times, and any chemical compatibility.

## Geometry and inputs

A shallow layer of ferrofluid in a container of stated dimensions, with a field generated by coils or permanent magnets whose geometry is a design variable. The target shapes are: a spike array at the critical wavelength, a ring, and a shaped mound. Inputs: material data (density, surface tension, `M(H)` curve), layer depth, and the field source layout.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A coupled magnetic–capillary free-surface solver in-process**: the magnetic body force and the magnetic pressure jump added to one of the existing interface solvers, with a verification case.
2. **The linear-stability reproduction**: a small module that computes the critical magnetisation and fastest wavelength for the stated fluid and depth, and compares them with the simulation's onset.
3. **A shape-error metric on the free surface**: a distance between the computed interface and the target profile, robust to the interface's numerical roughness.
4. **A field-source optimiser** with a manufacturability constraint on the coil or magnet layout.
5. **A controllability boundary**: the map of shapes reachable within the fluid's magnetisation limit and the stability margin.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify the field solver against an analytic coil field; report the error.
2. Reproduce the Rosensweig onset: sweep magnetisation at fixed layer depth, measure the onset and the dominant wavelength from the interface spectrum, compare against the analytic values; repeat at two depths.
3. Optimise the field for each target shape under the stability margin and the magnetisation limit.
4. Report the controllability boundary by sweeping target amplitude and spatial frequency.
5. Compute budget: interface-resolved runs are expensive — order 10^2–10^3 CPU-hours for the campaign; the stability study is the cheap part and should come first.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Analytic onset and wavelength for the Rosensweig instability: the primary reference.
- A flat interface with no field (zero deformation) and with a uniform field below the critical value.
- Mesh and time-step convergence on the onset case, with the onset magnetisation and the wavelength as observables.
- Volume conservation of the fluid during the deformation, as a hard check.

## Metrics

- Onset magnetisation, mT, and the most-unstable wavelength, mm, against the analytic values.
- Shape error to target: mean and maximum normal distance, mm.
- Achieved amplitude versus spatial frequency (the controllability map).
- Stability margin: ratio of applied to critical magnetisation at the reported shape.
- Field energy, mJ, required to hold the shape.

## Deliverables

- The coupled solver and its verification case.
- The stability reproduction, as a table of simulated versus analytic onset and wavelength.
- The controllability map and the optimised field layouts.
- A render of the spike pattern and one shaped surface with the field shown, from the campaign video pipeline.

## Acceptance criteria

- The simulated onset and wavelength match the analytic values within the stated tolerance at two layer depths.
- Volume is conserved within the stated tolerance in every accepted run.
- Every reported shape is held at a stability margin above a stated minimum, and the required field stays within the fluid's magnetisation limit.
- No shape is reported as controllable without appearing in the sweep; the map's boundaries are measured, not extrapolated.
- The field energy needed to hold each shape is reported beside the shape.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for ferrofluid shaping (used industrially), `extrapolated` for arbitrary target shapes. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (analytic onset, volume conservation), `numerical_stability` (mesh/time-step convergence on the onset), `physical_plausibility` (wavelength spectrum, magnetisation limit), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (material data spread). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed without a bench measurement, though published onset data could fill it and should be sought; `technological_feasibility` stays not assessed.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The interface solver smears the surface** and the wavelength measurement becomes a numerical artefact. Mitigation: measure the wavelength from a spectral peak with its resolution stated, and run two resolutions.
2. **A field that produces the shape also saturates the fluid**, so the result is outside the material's range. Mitigation: the magnetisation limit is a hard constraint.
3. **Target shapes chosen to be reachable.** Mitigation: the sweep defines the map before the targets are scored.
4. **Agglomeration ignored**, making the fluid more responsive than it is. Mitigation: state the assumption and use the measured `M(H)` curve rather than a Langevin fit alone.
5. **A striking image with no stability margin.** Mitigation: margin reported per shape; shapes below the minimum are excluded.

## Out of scope

- Long-term stability, particle settling and surfactant chemistry.
- Coil design, power electronics and thermal management.
- Ferrofluid seals or dampers as products.
