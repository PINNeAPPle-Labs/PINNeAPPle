## Summary

A shell of structured material surrounds a target and reduces the acoustic pressure it scatters over a stated frequency band. The experiment solves the Helmholtz equation on the exterior region with the shell, optimises the material distribution with physical bounds, and reports the scattering reduction as a function of frequency together with the material parameters the design demands. The band limitation is the result, not a caveat: a passive linear cloak is narrow-band, and the question is how narrow, and at what cost in density and bulk modulus.

## Question

Over what band, centred where, can a passive shell of bounded material properties reduce the acoustic scattering cross-section of a target by a stated factor, and which material parameter — density, bulk modulus or anisotropy — must leave its physically attainable range first as the band is widened?

## Why run it

- Acoustic scattering reduction is a real engineering problem in sonar, in machinery quieting and in duct acoustics, and the honest answer — a narrow band at the price of extreme parameters — is directly useful to anyone who has read a transformation-acoustics paper and wondered what it costs.
- The wave-propagation precedent already exists in the library in one dimension, so the work is a genuine extension to two and three dimensions with radiation boundary conditions, not a greenfield build.
- The acceptance test is a single frequency sweep with band edges, which is a compact, checkable deliverable and a good fit for one issue.

## Physical model

Time-harmonic acoustics in an inhomogeneous medium:

    ∇·( (1/rho) ∇p ) + (omega^2 / K) p = 0

with `rho(x)` the density and `K(x)` the bulk modulus of the shell material, `omega = 2 pi f`. The scattered field is the total field minus the incident plane wave, and the exterior region requires a radiation condition. The metrics are the scattering cross-section `sigma_sc(f)`, computed from the far-field amplitude, and the transmission loss across the shell in the forward direction.

For the idealised transformation-acoustics cloak, the required parameters of a spherical shell of inner radius `a` and outer radius `b` are anisotropic and diverge at the inner boundary, with the radial density `rho_r → 0` and the radial bulk modulus falling as `(r-a)/r`. The index-matching approach instead requires an effective index close to the background while preserving impedance, which is what a real metamaterial can approximate over a limited band. The dimensionless groups are `k a` (the size parameter), `b/a` (the shell thickness ratio), and the contrast ratios `rho_shell/rho_0` and `K_shell/K_0`.

Validity: linear acoustics, no mean flow, no thermo-viscous boundary layers, no structural vibration of the shell itself. The model deliberately omits the elastic response of the shell — it treats the shell as an acoustic medium with effective parameters, which is the assumption a metamaterial design then has to earn, and this is the largest gap between the simulation and a fabricated object. The model also omits absorption in the shell; if a design relies on absorption rather than redirection, its performance is not assessed here and the report must say so.

## Geometry and inputs

Target: a rigid cylinder of radius `a` in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2} m and a rigid sphere of the same radii, for the two- and three-dimensional cases. Shell thickness ratio `b/a` in {1.2, 1.5, 2.0}. Material parameterisation: isotropic density and bulk modulus in a first campaign, then a five-parameter anisotropic shell (radial and tangential density and modulus, plus one off-diagonal coupling) in a second. The optimisation variable is the radial material profile, discretized into 10 layers, with an optional azimuthal modulation of two harmonics.

Frequency: `k a` from 0.1 to 5 in 60 steps, with the band of interest stated per case. Incident plane wave at 0, 45 and 90 degrees to the shell's axis of symmetry to separate the monopole, dipole and quadrupole responses.

## Solver and engine

- The Helmholtz solver is the piece to build: a finite-element or finite-difference discretisation of the scalar Helmholtz equation on a structured exterior domain, with either a perfectly matched layer or a Dirichlet-to-Neumann boundary, run in `pinneapple_simulation/numerical_solvers/helmholtz_acoustic.py`.
- Fallback for verification: `pinneapple_simulation.external_solvers.fenics` with a standard Helmholtz variational form and a PML, used to check the in-house discretisation on the bare cylinder case.
- The one-dimensional wave precedent, `pinneapple_analysis.inspection.acoustic`, provides the wave-equation discretisation conventions and the FFT-based spectral post-processing used for the far field.
- Material distribution optimisation: `pinneapple_design.topology` provides the SIMP machinery, filters and MMA/OC update, which are reused with a Helmholtz objective in place of the compliance objective; the objective is complex-valued, so the sensitivity path needs an adjoint for the Helmholtz operator.
- Recording and figures: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`, `pp.dashboard`, `pinneapple_twin3d.scene` for the pressure field.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A 2-D and 3-D Helmholtz solver with a radiation boundary**: `pinneapple_simulation/numerical_solvers/helmholtz_acoustic.py` with `solve_helmholtz(domain, rho, K, k, incident, pml=)` returning the total field and the far-field amplitude. Radiation boundary conditions are the part that does not exist anywhere in the library today.
2. **A scattering metric layer**: `scattering_cross_section(field, k, incident)` and `transmission_loss(field, incident)` from the near field, with the far-field transformation validated against the analytic cylinder and sphere solutions.
3. **An adjoint sensitivity for the Helmholtz operator** with respect to the material field, so the topology optimizer can be used with a wave objective rather than a compliance one. The existing sensitivities in `pinneapple_design.topology` are for elliptic and Stokes problems.
4. **Physical bounds on the material parameters**, imposed as a constraint set and reported as a feasibility map: for each requested reduction and band, which parameter must exceed its bound.
5. **A band-edge search**: an outer loop over centre frequency and bandwidth that returns the widest band satisfying a stated reduction, given the material bounds. This is the experiment's headline output.
6. **A comparison with a homogeneous absorbing layer of equal thickness**, so the reader can see whether the structured shell beats the obvious alternative of the same mass.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify the Helmholtz solver: the analytic solution for a rigid cylinder and a rigid sphere in a plane wave, at three mesh resolutions, reporting the GCI on the scattering cross-section. Then verify against the FEniCS path on the same case.
2. Verify the far-field transformation: the computed cross-section must match the analytic partial-wave series within a stated tolerance up to `k a` of 5.
3. Unstructured shell campaign: isotropic parameter sweep over `rho_shell/rho_0` in {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2} and `K_shell/K_0` in the same set, 25 combinations, each an optimisation over the 10-layer radial profile at a single frequency, for three frequencies.
4. Bandwidth campaign: for each result from step 3, sweep the frequency and record the reduction curve; report the `-10 dB` band.
5. Anisotropic campaign: the five-parameter shell at the best frequency from step 4, reporting whether the anisotropic parameters improve the band and by how much.
6. Bounds analysis: for the best designs, report the required parameter values against the physical bounds, and the band that remains when the parameters are clamped to attainable values.
7. Compute budget: a 2-D solve is seconds to a minute; the optimisation needs 50 to 200 iterations per case — order 10^2 to 10^3 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The analytic partial-wave scattering series for the bare rigid cylinder and sphere is the reference; the solver must reproduce it before any shell result is quoted.
- A homogeneous shell of the same thickness and the same mean properties is the physical baseline the structured design must beat.
- An absorbing layer of equal thickness is the second baseline: if it performs comparably, the structured design's complexity is not justified and the report must say so.
- Reciprocity and energy checks: the scattered power computed from the far field must match the flux through a closed surface in the near field, within the numerical tolerance, for every case.
- Mesh refinement with GCI at three levels on the bare target and on one shell design.

## Metrics

- Scattering cross-section `sigma_sc(f)`, m², from the far-field amplitude, normalised by the geometric cross-section.
- Reduction `R(f) = sigma_sc,bare / sigma_sc,coated`, dB, as a function of frequency.
- Band `B_-10dB`, Hz or in `k a` units, the contiguous range where `R` exceeds 10 dB.
- Material contrast required, as ratios to the background, and the fraction of the profile outside the stated physical bounds.
- Transmission loss in the forward direction, dB, at the band centre.
- Energy residual: scattered power from the far field against the near-field flux, dimensionless, as a numerical-quality indicator.

## Deliverables

- The Helmholtz solver with the radiation boundary and the adjoint sensitivity, as importable modules with tests against the analytic targets.
- The reduction curves and the band table per case, as CSV.
- The material profile figures, with the physical-bound lines drawn on the same axes.
- The pressure-field renders at the band centre, bare versus coated, as images and as a scene in the viewer.
- A feasibility map: requested reduction against achievable band, with the binding material parameter labelled.
- A report whose first paragraph gives the band and the parameter cost, and states that the shell is an effective medium and not a fabricated structure.

## Acceptance criteria

- The solver reproduces the analytic bare-cylinder and bare-sphere cross-sections within 5 percent up to `k a` of 5 at the finest mesh, and the GCI is below 5 percent.
- The in-house solver and the FEniCS path agree on the same shell case within 3 percent on `sigma_sc`.
- The energy residual is below 1 percent for every reported case; a case that fails it is excluded and the exclusion is listed.
- Every reported band is accompanied by the material contrast it requires and by the band that remains after clamping to physical bounds.
- The structured design is compared against both baselines at equal thickness, and the report states plainly if it does not win.
- No band is reported for which the required parameters are outside the stated bounds without that fact appearing with the number.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` as a wave-scattering computation; `extrapolated` for a passive broadband cloak, because the transformation-acoustics solution requires parameters that diverge at the inner boundary and are not attainable; `fictional` for an omnidirectional cloak that works across a wide band without absorption. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (analytic targets, reciprocity and energy checks), `numerical_stability` (mesh convergence, PML reflection), `physical_plausibility` (parameter bounds of real acoustic metamaterials), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (sensitivity of the band to geometry and incidence angle). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed: no shell is built or measured in an acoustic tank here. `technological_feasibility` is only partly addressed, through the parameter-bound clamp.

To move up one class, in order: (1) a fabricated shell whose effective parameters are measured, and a measured reduction curve, since the effective-medium assumption is the whole distance between this simulation and a device; (2) a lossless material with density contrast below about 0.05 attainable over a real band, or an active element that replaces the parameter contrast with energy; (3) a shell thin enough to be practical, since the band and the thickness trade against each other; (4) operation under a non-plane-wave, reverberant field, which no narrow-band design can be expected to survive. Items 1 and 2 are the decisive ones.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The solver's radiation boundary reflects and manufactures a reduction.** Mitigation: the PML reflection is measured by solving the bare-target case with the target removed and comparing against the incident field alone; the residual is reported.
2. **The optimisation exploits a resonance in the shell rather than a broad reduction.** Mitigation: the reduction curve is reported over the full sweep, not at a single frequency, and the band width is an acceptance quantity.
3. **The effective-medium assumption is silently broken.** Mitigation: the report states the parameterisation and its limits, and compares one design against a resolvable microstructure at the finest mesh if compute permits, or declares that it was not done.
4. **The cross-section is computed from a near field that is too coarsely sampled.** Mitigation: the far-field transformation is validated against the analytic series before use, and the sampling density is a reported parameter.
5. **An absorbing design is credited as a cloaking design.** Mitigation: absorption in the shell is either absent from the model and stated, or included with the loss tangent reported per case.

## Out of scope

- Elastic and structural scattering, and the shell's own vibration.
- Fabrication, microstructure design and additive-manufacturing tolerance.
- Mean flow, turbulence and aeroacoustic sources.
- Underwater sonar performance prediction, which needs the ocean environment and is not modelled.
