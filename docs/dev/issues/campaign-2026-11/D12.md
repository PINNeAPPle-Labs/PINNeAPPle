## Summary

A building sits on adaptive isolators whose stiffness and damping are scheduled during the shaking, and the experiment asks how much peak floor acceleration that schedule buys against a set of recorded ground motions, relative to the passive isolation that already exists. The soil column and the building are coupled through a layered site response, the excitation is a set of real accelerograms, and the control law is restricted to what an implementable isolator can actually do. The report must state in the same breath that passive base isolation is established engineering and that only the adaptive part is the novelty.

## Question

Over a held-out set of recorded ground motions, what reduction in peak floor acceleration and inter-storey drift does a semi-active isolator schedule achieve relative to a passive isolator tuned to the same site, and how does that reduction degrade on motions that were not part of the schedule design?

## Why run it

- Base isolation is a mature field with an unresolved question in it: passive isolators are tuned to an assumed spectrum, and the interesting engineering claim is whether adaptation helps across a broad motion set or only for the motions used to design the schedule.
- The library carries earthquake data access and the structural solvers, so the experiment is a coupling problem plus a control study, both of which fit one issue.
- The negative result is as valuable as the positive: an adaptive schedule that overfits its design motions and loses to a well-tuned passive isolator on held-out motions is a finding that a practitioner needs.

## Physical model

Two coupled subsystems. The soil is a layered column solved as a one-dimensional shear beam:

    rho(z) u_tt = ( G(z) u_z )_z + rho(z) a_bedrock(t)

with strain-dependent modulus and a stated damping ratio per layer (a equivalent-linear site response is adequate and is what the experiment uses). The building is a lumped-mass shear frame with `N` degrees of freedom:

    M x_ddot + C x_dot + K x = -M 1 (a_surface(t) + ...)

coupled to the isolation layer through a nonlinear isolator element whose stiffness `k_i(t)` and damping `c_i(t)` follow a commanded schedule, subject to the physical constraint that a real isolator can change its properties only within a bounded range and at a bounded rate. That rate bound is the constraint that separates an implementable schedule from a fictional one.

Dimensionless groups: the ratio of the isolation frequency to the dominant site frequency, the isolation period relative to the superstructure period, the drift ratio at the isolation layer, and the ratio of the maximum achievable property change to the required change. Validity: the structure stays linear, which is a real limitation — a base-isolated building under a strong motion can enter the nonlinear range of its own frame, and that case is outside this model and must be stated. Soil nonlinearity is handled through equivalent-linear degradation with an iteration on the strain level, which is an approximation with a known bias.

## Geometry and inputs

Site profiles: a soft layer of 20 m over rock, a deep soft layer of 60 m over rock, and a stiff site of 8 m over rock, each with a stated shear-wave velocity profile. Building: 5, 10 and 15 storeys, uniform storey mass and stiffness, 3 m storey height. Isolator: a bilinear or a variable-stiffness element with a stiffness range from 0.4 to 2.5 times the nominal and a damping range from 2 to 25 percent, with a property change rate limit expressed in milliseconds.

Ground motions: a set of 30 recorded accelerograms with response spectra covering a range of magnitudes and distances, split into 20 design motions and 10 held-out motions. The split is fixed before the optimisation and the held-out set is not touched until the final evaluation.

## Solver and engine

- Soil column: a one-dimensional shear-beam solver, built on `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.fdm` with the equivalent-linear iteration in the experiment code.
- Structure: `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.beam_fe` for the frame and `plane_stress_fem` for a check on one frame against a lumped-mass model; the lumped model itself is a small explicit or Newmark integration written in the experiment module.
- Data: `pinneapple_data.datasets.external_sources.designsafe` for the hazard and structural data access, with the file provenance recorded through `pinneapple_data.lineage`.
- Optimisation of the schedule: `pinneapple_design.design_optimizer` for the global search over the schedule family, with a direct transcription of the stiffness and damping commands in time.
- Margins: `pinneapple_analysis.uncertainty.margin.margin_study` and `corner_stackup` over soil velocity and damping.
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A layered site-response model with equivalent-linear iteration**: `pinneapple_simulation/geophysics/site_response.py` with `column_response(profile, motion, strain_iterations=)` returning the surface accelerogram and the layer strains, plus the modulus reduction and damping curves as data. This does not exist; `geophysics` carries `richards`, `energy_balance` and `sphere_shallow_water` and nothing for shear-wave site response.
2. **A soil-structure coupled time integrator** that runs the soil column and the structure in one loop with the interface force at the foundation, rather than in two uncoupled passes, because the coupling is what makes the isolation result meaningful.
3. **A physically constrained isolator element**: a two-state model with a stiffness range, a damping range, a force capacity and a property change rate limit, and a hard refusal when a commanded schedule exceeds any of them. The rate limit is the piece that keeps the optimiser honest.
4. **A semi-active control law family** with a small parameter set — for example a skyhook-damping variant with a gain and a switching threshold, plus a clipped-optimal form — so the optimiser tunes a law that can be implemented rather than a free-form time series.
5. **A motion set manager** with the design/held-out split fixed in advance and the split recorded in the manifest, so the held-out evaluation cannot be contaminated by the design loop.
6. **A passivity check** on the isolator model: the energy removed by the isolator over the record must equal the integral of the damping force times velocity, and no schedule may inject energy.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify the site-response model against a published linear transfer function for a two-layer profile, and against a recorded downhole array if the dataset permits; report the transfer function amplitude against the analytic one.
2. Verify the structure model: natural periods of the 5, 10 and 15 storey frames against the closed-form shear-building values, and the response to a sinusoidal base motion against the analytic steady state.
3. Establish passive baselines per site and building: a fixed isolator tuned to the design spectrum, and one detuned by 30 percent, plus a fixed-base case. These are the numbers the adaptive schedule must beat.
4. Design the adaptive schedule on the 20 design motions, with the control law parameters optimised over the set, for each site and building combination: 3 sites by 3 buildings by 2 law families = 18 optimisations.
5. Held-out evaluation: run the tuned laws and the passive baselines on the 10 held-out motions, once, and report all quantities on that set.
6. Robustness: corner stackup over soil shear velocity, layer depth and damping; report the cases where the adaptive law loses to the passive baseline.
7. Compute budget: seconds per time-history run, thousands of runs in the optimisation — order 10^1 to 10^2 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The analytic transfer function of a layered elastic column is the site-model reference; the analytic steady-state response of a shear building under harmonic base motion is the structure reference.
- The fixed-base response and the passive-isolated response are the two engineering baselines. The adaptive law is judged against the passive isolator, never against the fixed base, since the fixed base is not a candidate design.
- The isolator energy accounting is a conservation check on every run: energy in from the ground motion equals energy dissipated plus kinetic plus strain energy plus the isolator's removed energy, to a stated tolerance.
- Monotonic consistency: with the property range set to zero, the semi-active law must reproduce the passive result exactly, which is a strong code check.
- Held-out superiority must be shown on the held-out set, and the design-set performance is reported separately so the overfitting is visible as a gap.

## Metrics

- Peak floor acceleration, m/s², maximum over floors and time, per motion, and its distribution over the 10 held-out motions.
- Peak inter-storey drift ratio, dimensionless, per motion.
- Isolator displacement, m, peak, as a feasibility constraint since a real isolator has a stroke limit.
- Reduction relative to the passive baseline, percent, in peak floor acceleration and in drift, per motion and as a median with the interquartile range.
- Design-to-held-out gap: the difference between the reduction on the design set and on the held-out set, as a direct overfitting measure.
- Energy removed by the isolator, J, against the input energy, as a mechanism indicator.

## Deliverables

- The site-response module and the coupled soil-structure integrator with their verification tests.
- The constrained isolator element and the two control law families as importable code.
- The full results table over 30 motions by 18 designs, as CSV, with the design/held-out split marked in the file.
- Figures: the response spectra of the motions, the storey-acceleration profiles for adaptive against passive for one motion per case, the design-to-held-out scatter, and the time histories of the commanded properties against their rate limits.
- A `pinneapple_lab` manifest per configuration, with the motion identifiers, the split and the seed.
- A report that states in its first paragraph that passive isolation is established and that only the adaptive schedule is under test.

## Acceptance criteria

- The site model reproduces the analytic two-layer transfer function within 5 percent in amplitude over the frequency band where the comparison is defined.
- The structure model reproduces the closed-form shear-building periods within 2 percent.
- The zero-range degeneracy check passes: a schedule constrained to a single property value reproduces the passive result to within numerical tolerance.
- The energy accounting closes within 1 percent on every reported run, and any run that fails is excluded and listed.
- The adaptive law beats the tuned passive isolator on the median held-out peak floor acceleration by a reported margin, or the report states that it does not and reports the deficit as the result.
- The design-to-held-out gap is reported as a number for every case, together with the number of held-out motions on which the adaptive law loses.
- No schedule is reported that violates the property range, the rate limit or the stroke limit; the optimiser's attempted violations are counted.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for passive base isolation, which is in service and codified; `extrapolated` for semi-active isolation with a scheduled property change, which exists in research and in some hardware but has no broad service record; `hypothetical` for a control law that anticipates the motion before it arrives without a measured early-wave signal. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (energy closure, degenerate case, analytic transfer function), `numerical_stability` (time-step convergence, equivalent-linear iteration convergence), `physical_plausibility` (property ranges of real isolators, stroke limits, rate limits), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (soil and damping corner stackup, design-to-held-out gap). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed: nothing is tested on a shake table here. `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the property, stroke and rate constraints.

To move up one class, in order: (1) a shake-table or field demonstration of a variable-property isolator with the achieved property change and switching time measured, since the rate limit is the deciding physical constraint; (2) a controller that operates on measured ground motion at the site with the sensing latency included, because a schedule that knows the whole record in advance is not implementable and this experiment gives it that knowledge; (3) a structure that enters its own nonlinear range, which is what a strong motion actually does; (4) long-term reliability of a mechanism that must sit dormant for decades and work once. Item 2 is the largest gap between this model and a deployed system, and the report must say so.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The optimiser exploits knowledge of the whole ground motion.** Mitigation: the control law is restricted to causal, current-measurement forms with a small parameter set; the free-form time-series schedule is explicitly not allowed, and the restriction is stated.
2. **Overfitting to the design motions is reported as performance.** Mitigation: the split is fixed in advance, the held-out set is evaluated once, and the design-to-held-out gap is a required metric.
3. **The comparison with passive isolation is at unequal displacement demand.** Mitigation: the isolator stroke is compared between the two, and a case where the adaptive law uses more stroke is reported as such rather than as a clean win.
4. **Equivalent-linear site response is used outside its range.** Mitigation: the effective strain is reported per motion, and cases with an effective strain above a stated limit are flagged.
5. **Soil-structure coupling is skipped and the two subsystems are run in sequence.** Mitigation: one case is run both coupled and uncoupled, and the difference is reported; if it is large, the uncoupled results are withdrawn.

## Out of scope

- Nonlinear behaviour of the superstructure, including yielding and P-delta effects.
- Foundation uplift, rocking and pile-soil interaction beyond a fixed-base assumption at the column base.
- Hardware design of the variable-property isolator, and its fatigue and ageing.
- Design codes, performance-based design procedures and any code-compliance claim.
