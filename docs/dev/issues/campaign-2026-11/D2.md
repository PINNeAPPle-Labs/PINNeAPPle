## Summary

Liquid metal that splits into limbs, holds a shape and reforms, as in the film. The experiment takes the premise seriously and computes, in order, why it fails: surface tension dominates at the scale where a droplet is small, a free liquid cannot sustain a bending moment, and any shaping mechanism must produce a body force comparable with the capillary pressure. The expected verdict is `CONTRADICTED` as portrayed and `CONDITIONAL` for small-scale shaping without load — the issue exists to show which numbers force that.

## Question

At what scale and with what applied field can a liquid-metal mass be held in a non-spherical shape that transmits a useful force, and where is the boundary between "shaping" and "the liquid simply rounds up"?

## Why run it

- It is the campaign's clearest case of a spectacular premise meeting a hard physical constraint, and it produces the constraint as a number rather than as an opinion.
- Ferrofluids and liquid-metal magnetohydrodynamics are real; the study isolates exactly what they can and cannot do, which is more useful than either debunking or pretending.
- The result is a design table: material, scale, field, achievable force — reusable for any "morphing liquid" proposal.

## Physical model

**Capillarity.** Capillary length `ℓ_c = sqrt(γ/(ρg))`; for a liquid metal (γ of order 0.5 N/m, density of order 10^4 kg/m³) it is millimetres. Any free surface feature smaller than that is dominated by surface tension, and the pressure to hold it scales as `γ/R`.

**Shape holding.** A free liquid at rest with no applied field is a sphere below `ℓ_c`. To hold a non-spherical shape, the applied stress must match `γ/R`; a body force (magnetic, electric, acoustic) supplies it. The required field follows from the stress balance, and the issue computes it for each candidate mechanism.

**No bending moment.** A liquid cannot transmit a moment through a free surface; a limb can only push if it is pressurised or if the body force acts throughout its volume. This is the reason a liquid-metal arm cannot act like a solid one, and the issue states it explicitly with the stress argument.

**Magnetohydrodynamics.** For a conducting liquid in a magnetic field, the induced currents and the Lorentz force shape the flow; the Hartmann number sets how much the field suppresses motion, and the interaction parameter sets the ratio of magnetic to inertial forces.

**Deliberately out of scope:** alloy chemistry, oxide-skin mechanics beyond a stated surface yield stress, and any claim about a machine.

## Geometry and inputs

A liquid-metal volume on a substrate or in a container, with a field source (coils or electrodes) whose geometry is a design variable; stated material data: density, surface tension, viscosity, electrical conductivity, and an oxide surface yield stress as a design input. Target shapes: a droplet, a short column, a two-lobed form, and a tool-like protrusion.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A magnetohydrodynamic free-surface solver**: the Lorentz force in the momentum equation with a conducting liquid and a free surface, verified on a known case (a conducting droplet in a uniform field, or the Hartmann channel flow).
2. **A shape-holding calculator**: the field required to hold a stated feature size, from the stress balance, as a function of material and scale — the analytic core of the issue.
3. **A force-transmission bound**: the maximum force a liquid form can transmit as a function of pressure and body force, stated as an inequality.
4. **An oxide-skin model** with a surface yield stress, because the skin is what lets real liquid metal hold any shape at all, and ignoring it overstates the liquid's freedom.
5. **The failure map**: for each target shape, the scale and field at which the shape is lost.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the free-surface solver on a sessile droplet (analytic cap shape) and on a Rayleigh–Plateau breakup; report the error.
2. Validate the MHD coupling on Hartmann channel flow against the analytic profile.
3. Compute the shape-holding stress balance and compare with the simulation's outcome for each target shape at three scales (millimetre, centimetre, decimetre).
4. Run the failure map: sweep field strength and feature size, record whether the shape holds.
5. Compute budget: interface-resolved runs dominate — order 10^1–10^2 CPU-hours; the analytic calculator runs in seconds and is done first.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Analytic capillary shapes (sessile droplet, Rayleigh–Plateau) as the solver validation.
- Hartmann flow analytic profile for the MHD validation.
- The equivalent solid limb, from `solid_fem`, as the reference for what a solid can do with the same volume and material — the comparison that decides the verdict.
- The stress-balance inequality as a check on every simulation result.

## Metrics

- Required stress and required field to hold a feature, versus feature size, per material.
- Achievable shape deviation from spherical, as a fraction of feature radius, per applied field.
- Transmitted force, N, versus the solid-limb reference, per unit volume.
- Hartmann number and interaction parameter per case, dimensionless.
- Verdict: physics `CONTRADICTED`/`CONDITIONAL`, engineering `NOT FEASIBLE`/`CONDITIONAL`, per claim.

## Deliverables

- The MHD free-surface solver and its verification cases.
- The analytic shape-holding calculator and the force-transmission bound.
- The failure map and the solid-comparison table.
- A render of one shaped state and its collapse, with the field shown, from the campaign video pipeline.
- An `pp.futures` assessment with the two verdicts and the axes each one rests on.

## Acceptance criteria

- The free-surface solver reproduces the analytic droplet shape and the Rayleigh–Plateau wavelength within the stated tolerance.
- The MHD coupling reproduces the Hartmann profile within the stated tolerance.
- Every reported shape satisfies the stress balance, and the required field is reported.
- The comparison with the equivalent solid limb is present in the report, with the force ratio as a number.
- The report states explicitly which part of the film's behaviour is `CONTRADICTED` and on what evidence.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `fictional` as portrayed; `extrapolated` for constrained shaping at small scale with a strong field. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (analytic droplet, Hartmann profile, stress balance), `numerical_stability` (interface resolution convergence), `physical_plausibility` (capillary length, absence of bending moment, oxide skin), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (material data spread). `experimental_validation` stays not assessed (and real liquid-metal shaping is measured in the literature, which should be cited); `technological_feasibility` stays not assessed, though the force bound is its form.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **Interface-resolution artefacts read as physical instability.** Mitigation: resolution convergence on the collapse case.
2. **The oxide skin is ignored**, making the liquid look freer than it is. Mitigation: surface yield stress is a stated input and its effect is shown.
3. **The verdict is asserted rather than computed.** Mitigation: the stress balance and the solid comparison are the evidence, and both are numbers.
4. **The MHD coupling is unverified** and the whole shaping result is built on it. Mitigation: the Hartmann validation is an acceptance criterion.
5. **Overreaching the other way** — declaring all liquid shaping impossible when ferrofluids and liquid-metal jets do shape. Mitigation: the verdict is per claim, not global.

## Out of scope

- Alloy development and melting/solidification.
- A machine, a robot or a vehicle.
- Biological or medical application.
