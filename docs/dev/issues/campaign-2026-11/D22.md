## Summary

A dynamical system that returns to a neighbourhood of its own initial state at fixed intervals: a reset map applied to a nonlinear system. The experiment maps what the closed loop does — fixed point, period doubling, chaos, collapse to a different attractor — as a function of how much of the state is reset and how often. It is explicitly a mathematical analogy for loops in narrative and in iterative design, and says so on every page.

## Question

For a chaotic or excitable system with a partial reset applied every `T`, which resets stabilise a periodic orbit, which leave the dynamics unchanged, and which destroy the attractor altogether?

## Why run it

- It is cheap (single-processor, minutes per run) and it produces a bifurcation diagram, which is a real result rather than an illustration.
- The pattern — a loop that intervenes to fix an error and thereby changes the conditions of the next cycle — is the honest mathematical content behind time-loop stories, and the campaign needs at least one issue where the discipline of the analogy is the point.
- The machinery transfers to iterative design loops, where the same question appears as "does re-running the optimiser with feedback converge or oscillate".

## Physical model

**Base systems.** A chaotic ODE (Lorenz-type), a chaotic map, and an excitable system (a relaxation oscillator being the simplest). The reset is a map `R` applied to the state at times `kT`: either a full reset to the initial condition plus noise, or a partial reset of a subset of the variables.

**Measured behaviour.** The Lyapunov exponent of the closed loop, the return map at the reset instants, the distribution of return states, and the fate of the attractor (stable periodic, chaotic, escaping to a different basin).

**Dimensionless controls.** Reset period over the system's characteristic timescale, fraction of the state reset, and reset noise amplitude.

**Deliberately out of scope:** any relativistic or causal structure. This is a map on a state space, not a spacetime.

## Geometry and inputs

No geometry; the inputs are the base system, the initial condition, the reset rule and its parameters, and the integration accuracy. The initial condition distribution is sampled over a stated set so the result is not a single-trajectory anecdote.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A reset-map framework**: a wrapper that integrates, applies the reset, and records the state at every reset instant, with the integration accuracy verified per segment.
2. **A return-map analyser**: the map at reset instants, its fixed points and their stability, and the bifurcation sweep over reset period and fraction.
3. **A Lyapunov estimator for the closed loop**, distinguishing the flow's exponent from the reset's effect.
4. **A basin-of-attraction check**: whether a reset pushes the state into a different basin, which is the failure mode that makes the loop destructive.
5. **A sensitivity study** on the reset noise amplitude, since noise may stabilise or destroy the periodic orbit.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the integrator on the base system: Lyapunov exponent of the unreset flow against the literature value, within the stated tolerance.
2. Sweep the reset period at fixed reset fraction; record the fate of the attractor per run and the return-map structure.
3. Repeat over the reset fraction and over several initial conditions per parameter point.
4. Add noise at three amplitudes and repeat the sweep for one parameter slice.
5. Compute budget: minutes per trajectory, thousands of trajectories for the diagram — order 10^1 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The unreset system: its Lyapunov exponent and attractor are the reference for "nothing changed".
- Literature values for the base systems' Lyapunov exponents.
- Integration-accuracy check: halving the step must not change the reported exponent beyond its interval.
- Seed and initial-condition repeats, reported as intervals rather than single values.

## Metrics

- Lyapunov exponent of the closed loop, per time unit, with its interval.
- Periodic-orbit period, in reset units, where one is stabilised.
- Return-state dispersion: standard deviation of the state at reset instants, dimensionless.
- Attractor fate: categorical (same attractor, periodic orbit, different basin, escape), counted over initial conditions.
- Sensitivity of the fate to reset noise amplitude, reported as a fraction of parameter space.

## Deliverables

- The reset framework and the return-map analyser with tests.
- The bifurcation diagrams as data and figures, with the fate categories marked.
- The validation of the base-system exponents.
- A short animation of one stabilising and one destructive loop, from the campaign video pipeline.

## Acceptance criteria

- The base-system Lyapunov exponent matches the literature within the stated tolerance before any reset result is reported.
- Every parameter point is evaluated over at least ten initial conditions and reported with a dispersion measure, not as a single trajectory.
- Integration accuracy: the reported exponent is stable under step halving.
- The report states, in the summary and in the results section, that this is a state-space analogy and makes no claim about time travel.
- Failure outcomes (different basin, escape) are reported with the same standing as stabilisation.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `fictional` for a physical time loop; `established` for the mathematics of reset maps. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (Lyapunov validation, integration accuracy), `numerical_stability` (step and seed convergence), `physical_plausibility` (attractor fate is a well-defined dynamical property), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (noise sweep). `experimental_validation` and `technological_feasibility` are not assessed and cannot be, which is stated in the issue's own summary.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The analogy is read as a physical claim.** Mitigation: the disclaimer is in the summary, the results and the figure captions.
2. **Single-trajectory anecdotes.** Mitigation: ten initial conditions per point, dispersion reported.
3. **Numerical chaos mistaken for physical behaviour.** Mitigation: step-halving check and a stated integration tolerance.
4. **Too cheap to be taken seriously.** Mitigation: the deliverable is a diagram over a parameter sweep with intervals, which is a real artefact.

## Out of scope

- Spacetime, relativity and closed timelike curves (a separate issue covers the metric side).
- Applications to control design, beyond the stated analogy.
