## Summary

Reconstructing a past state from what is left of it: sparse, noisy measurements taken later are inverted to recover an earlier field, with a reported uncertainty. The physics is a backward parabolic problem, which is ill-posed — small errors in the data grow exponentially backwards — so the experiment is about regularisation, about how much information genuinely survives, and about the number of sensors at which the answer stops depending on them. It is a "window into the past" only in the sense that an inverse problem is, and the report says exactly what was and was not recovered.

## Question

For a diffusion (or advection–diffusion) field observed by `n` sensors at time `T`, how accurately can the field at time `t < T` be reconstructed as a function of `n`, the noise level and the regularisation strength, and where is the point at which more sensors stop helping?

## Why run it

- Ill-posed inverse problems are the honest core of "looking back in time", and the information-loss number is a result no animation can give.
- The library has the inverse-problem scaffolding (`identifiability`, uncertainty propagation) and the forward solvers, so this is a genuine integration exercise with a measurable outcome.
- It produces a practical artefact: given a sensor budget, how far back can you reconstruct — which transfers to process monitoring, thermal history reconstruction and leak source location.

## Physical model

**Forward problem.** `∂u/∂t = ∇·(D∇u) + f` on a stated domain with boundary conditions, solved forward from a known initial field; observations are point samples plus noise.

**Inverse problem.** Recover the initial field, or the state at a time in the past, from the observations. The operator's singular values decay exponentially, which is why the problem is ill-posed and why regularisation is the design variable.

**Regularisation.** Tikhonov with a parameter chosen by the discrepancy principle or generalised cross-validation, and — as a second method — a Bayesian posterior with a Gaussian process prior, so the two can be compared on the same metric.

**Dimensionless controls.** Fourier number (`Dt/L²`) at the reconstruction horizon, signal-to-noise ratio, and the sensor density relative to the field's dominant length scale.

**Deliberately out of scope:** nonlinear dynamics, radiative transfer, and any claim about recovering information that was never measured. This is reconstruction from existing data, not retrieval of lost data.

## Geometry and inputs

A 2-D and a 3-D domain with a stated initial field (a smooth blob and a two-blob case, so the reconstruction difficulty varies), a sensor layout (a grid and a random set, both counted), and a noise model. The "past event" is a synthetic ground truth, which makes the error measurable — the experiment never claims otherwise.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A backward/adjoint solver** with the regularisation term, and the choice rule for its parameter.
2. **A Bayesian alternative**: a Gaussian-process prior with a diffusion kernel, and a posterior sampler whose credible intervals are comparable with the deterministic reconstruction error.
3. **A sensor-count study**: reconstruction error versus `n`, including a sensor-placement comparison (grid versus optimised versus random).
4. **An information-loss metric**: the mutual information between the initial field and the observations under the stated prior, or the condition number of the discretised operator, so "how far back can you go" has a number.
5. **A stopping rule**: the horizon at which the reconstruction error exceeds the prior's own variance, i.e. where the data adds nothing.

## Experiment protocol

1. Validate the forward solver against an analytic solution (a Gaussian blob diffusing in an unbounded domain, or the separation-of-variables solution in a rectangle).
2. Reconstruct at a short horizon with dense sensors, where the problem is nearly well-posed, to verify the inverse machinery.
3. Extend the horizon in steps; at each horizon, sweep sensor count and noise; record the reconstruction error, the regularisation parameter chosen and the credible interval width.
4. Compare grid, random and actively selected sensor sets at equal `n`.
5. Report the stopping horizon per noise level.
6. Compute budget: each inversion needs repeated forward solves — order 10^2 CPU-hours for the sweep.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The ground-truth field (known by construction).
- A naive baseline: the final observation smeared back with the analytic kernel, and the prior mean with no data — the reconstruction must beat both.
- Forward-solver validation against the analytic solution.
- Regularisation parameter selection: independent of the error metric used to report the result, so the comparison is not circular.

## Metrics

- Relative L2 reconstruction error, dimensionless, and the maximum pointwise error, per horizon and sensor count.
- Credible/confidence interval width at the 90 % level, in field units, and its coverage of the truth.
- Stopping horizon: the Fourier number at which the error exceeds the prior variance.
- Sensor efficiency: error at equal `n` for grid, random and active placement.
- Condition number (or singular-value decay rate) of the discretised operator, per horizon.

## Deliverables

- The adjoint/regularised inverse solver and the Bayesian alternative, with tests.
- The forward-solver validation.
- Error-versus-horizon and error-versus-sensor-count curves, as data and figures, with interval coverage.
- An animation of the reconstruction at three horizons beside the truth, from the campaign video pipeline.
- A written statement of the stopping horizon and of what cannot be recovered.

## Acceptance criteria

- The forward solver reproduces the analytic solution within the stated tolerance, with the observed order of convergence.
- The inverse machinery recovers a short-horizon field to within the sensor noise level; if it does not, no long-horizon result is reported.
- Interval coverage at the 90 % level falls in a stated band (for example 85–95 %) across the sweep, or the miscalibration is reported.
- The stopping horizon is reported per noise level, and no reconstruction beyond it is presented as a result.
- The regularisation parameter selection never uses the error metric that the result is scored on.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for inverse reconstruction; `fictional` for recovering information that was never recorded. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (analytic forward check, adjoint gradient test against finite differences), `numerical_stability` (grid and horizon convergence, interval coverage), `physical_plausibility` (maximum principle respected by the reconstruction), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (the whole experiment is a sensitivity study). `experimental_validation` is not assessed; `technological_feasibility` is not assessed, though the sensor-count curve is its practical form.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **Circular validation**: the regularisation tuned on the same metric that reports success. Mitigation: independent selection rule, stated explicitly.
2. **Overconfident intervals** from a misspecified prior. Mitigation: coverage reported as an acceptance criterion.
3. **A "reconstruction" that is the prior mean.** Mitigation: the no-data baseline is reported beside every result.
4. **Sensor placement tested only where it flatters the method.** Mitigation: grid, random and active placement at equal counts.
5. **The result is read as recovering unrecorded information.** Mitigation: the stopping horizon and the information-loss metric are in the summary.

## Out of scope

- Nonlinear or chaotic dynamics, where backward integration fails for reasons beyond regularisation.
- Electromagnetic or seismic imaging (different operators, different priors).
- Hardware for the sensors.
