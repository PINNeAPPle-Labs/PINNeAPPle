## Summary

A sentence describes a part — "a bracket that holds 20 kg over a 200 mm span" — and the system turns the intent into a geometry, plans how a population of modular units assembles it, checks the assembled part mechanically, and reports whether it is feasible, how long the assembly takes and what is missing. The language is the interface; the physics is the referee. A request that cannot be met produces a refusal with the reason, not a plausible-looking object.

## Question

For a stated set of modular units and connectors, what fraction of realistic part requests can be turned into an assembly that passes a mechanical check, and what is the dominant reason the rest fail?

## Why run it

- It is the concrete version of "speak a part and matter forms it", with a measurable success rate instead of a demo.
- Every failure reason it collects (not enough units, no connector combination, load too high, geometry impossible) is a design requirement for the modular system, which makes the output useful even when the answer is negative.
- It integrates four things that exist separately in the library — intent parsing, generation, assembly statics, and refusal logic — which is exactly the kind of integration an issue should own.

## Physical model

**Mechanics of the assembled part.** Linear statics on the assembly: each unit a stiff body, each connector a joint with axial, shear and bending capacity; the part passes if the load case produces stresses and deflections inside stated limits with a factor.

**Assembly.** Units occupy positions on a lattice or a free arrangement; the planner must produce an order in which the partial assembly is stable at every step.

**Feasibility.** Three independent tests, all of which must pass: geometry (the target shape can be approximated by the available unit inventory), mechanics (the load case passes), and assembly (a stable order exists).

**Deliberately out of scope:** manufacturing tolerances, cost, and any physical actuation of the units; the units are abstract modules with stated mass, size and connector strength.

## Geometry and inputs

Target shapes come from a fixed prompt set (at least 20 parts: brackets, a shelf, a stool, a clamp, a hook, a truss segment) with stated loads and spans, written down before the experiment. The unit inventory is a design input: unit sizes, connector types, their strength and stiffness, and the total count available.

The intent layer may use `pinneapple_design.intent` and `pp.design(text)` templates, plus the language nodes in `pinneapple_flow.nodes_ext` and the LLM integration in `pinneapple_llm` — the choice is recorded, and a rule-based parser is the baseline the language model must beat.

## Solver and engine

- Intent → problem: `pp.design(text, answers=, out_dir=)`, `pinneapple_design.intent` (templates, questions, refusal), `pinneapple_flow.plan`.
- Geometry and assembly: `pinneapple_design.geometry.gen`, `pinneapple_design.bricks` (`Assembly`, `analyse_assembly`, `CantileverGoal`, `BridgeGoal`, `TowerGoal`, `evolve`, `surrogate_search`).
- Mechanics: the `bricks` statics plus `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.solid_fem`/`beam_fe` for the verification of the final assembly.
- Explanation of failure: `pinneapple_reason` (`reason`, `chain.explain`, `write_report`) for the mechanism of each refusal.
- Mission loop: `pinneapple_autolab` (`Mission`, `Simulator`, `assess_candidates`, `choose_next`).
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What already exists in PINNeAPPle (verified)

- `pp.design(text)` and `pinneapple_design.intent`: goal to verified design, the questions it needs, or a refusal stating what is missing.
- `pinneapple_design.bricks`: assembly statics with goals, search and comparison — the closest existing analogue to modular assembly.
- `pinneapple_reason`: naive concept → failure mode with its mechanism and numbers → countermeasure, and the written report.
- `pinneapple_autolab` for the mission loop; `pinneapple_analysis.benchmark` for scoring a fixed prompt set; `pinneapple_flow` for the node-level composition.
- `pinneapple_twin3d`/`pinneapple_blender` for the assembly animation.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **An assembly planner**: from a target mesh and the unit inventory to positions plus a stable build order, with the stability check at each intermediate step.
2. **A connector capacity model**: strength and stiffness per connector type, since `bricks` handles stud clutch limits but not a general capacity set.
3. **The three-test feasibility gate** as a single function returning pass/fail plus the governing constraint.
4. **A scoring harness over the fixed prompt set**: parse success, geometry success, mechanics success, assembly success, and the overall success rate — with the rule-based parser as baseline.
5. **A refusal generator** that names the governing constraint in physical terms (force in N, span in mm, units short by n), using `pinneapple_reason` rather than free text.

## Experiment protocol

1. Freeze the prompt set and its load cases before any run; record them in the manifest.
2. Run the rule-based baseline parser on all prompts; record parse and design success.
3. Run the language-model path with the same prompts; compare on the same metrics and record cost per prompt.
4. For every successful design, run the assembly planner and check intermediate stability.
5. For every failure, produce the governing constraint and confirm it by re-running with that constraint relaxed (a one-at-a-time sensitivity), so the stated reason is verified rather than asserted.
6. Compute budget: minutes per prompt and per assembly check — order 10^1 CPU-hours for the whole set.

## Baselines, references and verification

- Rule-based parser and template design as the baseline.
- A hand-designed assembly for three of the prompts, as the quality reference for what a good answer looks like.
- Failure-reason verification by relaxation: relaxing the named constraint must make the design feasible; if it does not, the stated reason was wrong.
- Intermediate-stability re-analysis of every assembly step, not just the final state.

## Metrics

- Overall success rate over the prompt set, and the breakdown by stage (parse, geometry, mechanics, assembly).
- Cost per prompt: tokens and wall-clock, for the language path versus the rule-based baseline.
- Assembly time, in unit placements, and the number of steps with a negative stability margin (must be zero).
- Refusal accuracy: the fraction of refusals whose named constraint is confirmed by relaxation.
- Structural margin of accepted designs: utilisation ratio against the stated limits.

## Deliverables

- The planner, the connector model, the feasibility gate and the refusal generator.
- The fixed prompt set with its results table, committed as a benchmark.
- The assembly animations for three accepted prompts, from the campaign video pipeline.
- A short report on the dominant failure reasons and what they imply for the modular system's design.

## Acceptance criteria

- Every accepted design passes all three tests, with the assembly stable at every intermediate step (re-solved, not interpolated).
- Every refusal names a constraint that is confirmed by the relaxation test.
- The prompt set and the load cases are unchanged from the manifest; any later addition is a separate, labelled run.
- The language path's cost and success rate are reported against the rule-based baseline; if it does not beat it, the report says so.
- No design is accepted on geometry alone.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `extrapolated` — modular assembly and intent-to-design exist separately and in research prototypes; a system that assembles arbitrary parts from a spoken request does not exist as a product. Axes: `mathematical_consistency` (statics checks, relaxation tests), `numerical_stability` (re-solved assembly steps), `physical_plausibility` (connector capacities, structural margins), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (one-at-a-time relaxation around each refusal). `experimental_validation` and `technological_feasibility` stay not assessed.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The prompt set is chosen to flatter the system.** Mitigation: frozen before the run, with parts that should fail included on purpose.
2. **The refusal is plausible prose with no physics.** Mitigation: relaxation test as an acceptance criterion.
3. **Planner output that is stable only at the end.** Mitigation: every intermediate state re-solved.
4. **Language-model cost hides a low success rate.** Mitigation: cost and success reported together.
5. **The connector model quietly allows superhuman joints.** Mitigation: capacities are stated design inputs with their provenance.

## Out of scope

- Physical actuation, self-assembly dynamics and control of the units (other issues cover assembly physics).
- Cost, procurement and manufacturing.
- General-purpose CAD generation beyond what the templates cover.
