## Summary

A population of small units clings to a load-bearing frame, and the frame is then damaged by removing a stated fraction of the units. The experiment asks whether the remaining units can reorganise to keep the load carried, and reports the fraction at which they cannot. Physics is the interesting part and it is ordinary: adhesion and per-unit force budgets, the global stability of the assembled form, and the reconfiguration path, which must not pass through a configuration that collapses. The result is either the surviving load fraction after a 20 percent loss or the failure fraction, and both are results.

## Question

For a frame assembled from `N` units with a stated adhesion and per-unit force budget, what is the largest fraction of units that can be removed while the remaining population still carries the design load, and what is the best reconfiguration sequence that achieves it?

## Why run it

- It is the clearest statement of what a swarm can and cannot do: the load path is a structural question and the swarm's coordination is a search question, and both have to be answered for the demonstration to mean anything.
- The failure fraction is a hard number that bounds every other claim about such swarms, and it is exactly the sort of result that a structural check can produce honestly.
- It reuses the assembly statics, the rigid-body units and the campaign loop that already exist, so the new content is the damage model, the coordination policy and the stability metric along the path.

## Physical model

Each unit is a rigid body with an adhesion model: a pairwise or face-based constraint with a stated tensile and shear capacity, and a per-unit actuator force budget for holding and moving. The assembled frame is a rigid-jointed truss whose members are unit-to-unit interfaces, and the structural problem is

    K u = f,  with member forces from u,  checked against capacity

for the equilibrium under the design load, plus a stability check on the joint configuration: the assembled form must be kinematically determinate or, if over-constrained, the redundancy must be distributed so that a single interface failure does not propagate. Damage is the removal of a set of units, which removes their interfaces and their load capacity together.

The reconfiguration path is a sequence of discrete steps; at every intermediate step the structure must satisfy the same equilibrium and capacity checks with the design load applied. That requirement is what makes the problem hard, and it is where real assembly plans fail.

Dimensionless groups: the load per unit relative to the adhesion capacity, the redundancy `m - 2j` relative to the number of members, and the damage fraction. Validity: rigid bodies, quasi-static loading, no dynamics during the reconfiguration, and no fatigue of the adhesion. The model cannot show the detachment and reattachment dynamics of a real unit, and it assumes the adhesion capacity is uniform, which real units will not have.

## Geometry and inputs

Frame geometries: a cantilever panel truss spanning 2 m with a 20 kg mid-span load; a bridge-like arch with a 50 kg distributed load; a spherical shell segment loaded at the pole. Unit counts from 40 to 200. Unit mass 0.5 kg, adhesion capacity from 20 to 200 N in tension and shear, actuator budget 5 N per unit. Damage scenarios: random removal of 5, 10, 20 and 33 percent of units, plus the worst-case removal set found by search, plus removal of complete structural groups (all units on one face).

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A unit adhesion and interface model** for a non-brick geometry: interfaces with tensile, shear and moment capacity, and a failure criterion that removes the interface when exceeded. `bricks` handles interlocking contact between identical blocks; a general unit with a strength-rated connector does not exist.
2. **A damage model that removes units physically**, taking their interfaces and their holding capacity with them, and updating the equilibrium problem rather than the geometry alone.
3. **A stability metric evaluated at every intermediate state** of a reconfiguration: the minimum capacity ratio over the path, the number of states that dip below one, and the load path continuity. Without this the reconfiguration claim is unverifiable.
4. **A distributed coordination policy**: a rule each unit can execute from local information only — which neighbours it sees and the load it senses — with a small parameter set, so the result is about a swarm and not about a centrally computed plan. The policy must be run with a communication range limit, since unlimited communication trivialises the problem.
5. **A robustness campaign driver** that runs the removal scenarios against the policy and the best centralised plan, and reports both, so the distributed penalty is a measured number.
6. **A refusal rule**: a configuration whose remaining units cannot carry the load under any sequence is reported with the fraction and the failing member, not optimised into a marginal pass.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify the assembly statics against `bricks.analyse_assembly` on a brick-shaped test case, where both models apply, and against the closed-form truss solution for a simple determinate frame.
2. Undamaged baselines: each geometry assembled and loaded, reporting the member force distribution and the minimum capacity ratio.
3. Damage sweep: 3 geometries by 4 damage fractions by 3 removal patterns (random with 20 seeds, worst-case by search, group removal) = 36 cells, each evaluated without reconfiguration first, to establish the static loss.
4. Reconfiguration: for the cells that fail statically, run the policy search for a sequence, with the stability check at every step; report the best sequence found and the number of steps.
5. Distributed against centralised: the same cases with the local-rule policy under a stated communication radius, against the centralised plan, reporting the success-rate difference.
6. Worst-case damage: search for the removal set that fails at the lowest fraction, and report that fraction as the headline.
7. Compute budget: seconds per equilibrium solve, thousands of solves in the search — order 10^1 to 10^2 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The closed-form determinate truss and the `bricks` model are the two statics references.
- The undamaged minimum capacity ratio is the reference against which the damaged ratio is expressed.
- A no-reconfiguration baseline (the damaged structure as it stands) and a centralised-oracle baseline (the best sequence found by full search) bracket the distributed policy's performance; both are reported.
- Conservation check: the sum of member forces at each joint balances the applied load to numerical tolerance, at every step of every sequence.
- The stability metric's path requirement is verified by a deliberately bad sequence, which must be rejected by the metric. If the metric accepts it, the metric is wrong.

## Metrics

- Load-carrying capacity after damage, as a fraction of the undamaged capacity, dimensionless.
- Failure fraction `f_fail`, the removed fraction at which no sequence carries the load.
- Minimum capacity ratio along the best sequence, dimensionless, and the number of intermediate states below one.
- Reconfiguration steps and total unit displacement, as a cost measure.
- Distributed-to-centralised success ratio, dimensionless, at a stated communication radius.
- Worst-case removal set size, as the adversarial bound.

## Deliverables

- The unit interface model, the damage model, the path stability metric and the coordination policy as importable code with the statics tests.
- The damage sweep table with capacity fractions, as CSV.
- The best sequences as step-by-step renders and a video from the campaign pipeline, with the capacity ratio annotated per step.
- The worst-case removal set visualised on the frame, with the failing member identified.
- A `pinneapple_lab` manifest per run, with seeds, geometry and policy parameters.
- A report whose first paragraph gives the failure fraction, whether it is above or below 20 percent.

## Acceptance criteria

- The statics model reproduces the closed-form determinate truss to within 1 percent and agrees with `bricks.analyse_assembly` on the brick case to within the model difference, which is reported.
- The joint equilibrium residual is below 1e-9 relative at every step of every reported sequence.
- The path stability metric rejects a deliberately invalid sequence; a metric that accepts it fails the issue.
- Every reported sequence has its minimum capacity ratio above one in every intermediate state, or the sequence is reported as invalid and not as a result.
- The distributed policy is evaluated under a stated finite communication radius, and the centralised baseline is reported alongside it.
- The report gives the failure fraction for both random and worst-case damage, and does not report only the favourable one.
- No configuration is reported as surviving without the failing-member analysis for the cases where it does not.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for the statics of a jointed frame and for reconfiguration as a search problem; `extrapolated` for a swarm of the stated size with the stated adhesion, since the per-unit force budget and the adhesion-to-mass ratio are the binding engineering quantities and are assumed here; `hypothetical` for a self-healing structural swarm operating without central control in a real environment. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (equilibrium residual, truss reference, metric validation), `numerical_stability` (search repeatability across seeds, solver conditioning under damage), `physical_plausibility` (adhesion capacity and per-unit force against real small-robot values), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (damage pattern, capacity spread). `experimental_validation` is not assessed: no units are built. `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the force budget.

To move up one class, in order: (1) units with a measured adhesion capacity and a measured detachment-reattachment time, since both are assumed and both bound the achievable reconfiguration; (2) a coordination policy demonstrated on physical units under a real communication constraint, because the simulated policy assumes perfect state knowledge within the radius; (3) a dynamic analysis of the reconfiguration, since the quasi-static assumption hides the transient that would actually break the frame; (4) failure tolerance for a partially failed unit rather than a cleanly removed one, which is what damage actually looks like.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The stability metric is too permissive and the sequences it blesses would collapse.** Mitigation: the metric is validated against a deliberately bad sequence, and the residual check runs at every step.
2. **The centralised baseline makes the distributed policy look adequate when it is not.** Mitigation: both are reported, and the communication radius is a stated parameter with the policy's sensitivity to it shown for at least two radii.
3. **The worst-case search finds a pathological removal set that is not physically meaningful.** Mitigation: the search is constrained to connected removal sets and the group-removal scenarios are reported alongside, so the reader can separate adversarial from plausible.
4. **The quasi-static assumption hides the real failure.** Mitigation: one case is run with a short dynamic relaxation between steps, and the difference is reported.
5. **The failure fraction is buried and only the success case is shown.** Mitigation: the headline metric is the failure fraction, and it is required for both damage patterns.

## Out of scope

- Unit hardware design, power, sensing and communication implementation.
- Dynamics of detachment and reattachment, and the control of an individual unit.
- Space environment effects, thermal cycling and radiation.
- Any claim about self-repair of a real structure in service.
