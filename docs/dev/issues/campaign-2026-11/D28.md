## Summary

One inventory of modules reorganises into three tools — a wrench, a gripper and a gear — and the experiment checks, for each, the function that actually matters: transmitted torque at the fastener, grip force on the object, and tooth load at the mesh. The same modules and the same total mass serve all three, so the question is whether a single set can deliver any of them well, and the answer is expected to be that it cannot deliver all three. The report must include the tool at which the modules fail, since that is what bounds how universal the set can be.

## Question

For one module inventory of stated mass and connector strength, what fraction of the required wrench torque, grip force and gear tooth load can be transmitted by the three assembled tools, and which of the three fails first?

## Why run it

- "Universal tool" is a claim about a trade, and the trade has a concrete form: the same modules must act as a lever arm, a parallel jaw and a toothed wheel, and each has a different load path. Making all three from one inventory exposes which geometry is the compromise.
- The kinetostatic analysis of each tool is textbook mechanics with closed-form checks, so every number can be verified, and the experiment is a design-space study rather than a simulation build.
- The failure case is the deliverable, and it is directly useful: it says which function a modular inventory should not be asked to perform.

## Physical model

Each tool is a mechanism made of the same stiff modules, so the model is kinetostatics plus member strength.

**Wrench.** A lever of length `L` with a handle and a socket interface. The transmitted torque is `T = F_applied L`, and the member that carries it is the handle in bending, `sigma = M c / I`, with the socket interface carrying the reaction as a shear and a bearing pressure. The limit is the smaller of the member strength and the interface capacity, and the interesting regime is where the interface binds first.

**Gripper.** A parallel-jaw or linkage gripper with a transmission ratio `r = d_jaw / d_actuator`. The grip force is `F_grip = r F_actuator` minus losses, and the constraint is that the same modules acting as the linkage must also carry the reaction without buckling. Contact at the jaw is modelled with a stated friction coefficient and a Hertzian or soft-contact pressure, since the object's surface pressure is a constraint as real as the force.

**Gear.** A module ring with `N` teeth, a stated module (the tooth-size parameter, not the package), and a transmitted tangential load `F_t = 2T/d`. The tooth bending stress follows the Lewis form factor and the contact stress the Hertzian line-contact expression; the limits are tooth breakage and pitting.

The cross-cutting constraint that makes the problem hard: the total module mass is fixed across the three tools, so the wrench's long handle competes with the gear's ring and the gripper's linkage for the same modules. The trade is a mass allocation.

Dimensionless groups: the load per module relative to the connector capacity, the ratio of the tool's characteristic length to the module size, and the mass fraction allocated to each function. Validity: small-deformation statics, no dynamics, no wear, no fatigue, and a rigid-jointed frame. The model cannot show the assembly time, the accuracy of the connection, or the compliance between modules, which for a real modular tool is often the limiting factor and is stated as an omission.

## Geometry and inputs

Module inventory: 60 modules of 0.15 kg each, 40 mm characteristic size, connector capacity 300 N in tension, 150 N in shear and 8 N·m in moment, stiff enough that the connector compliance is small but not zero. Three targets, each stated as a requirement with a tolerance: a wrench delivering 60 N·m at a 250 mm handle; a gripper delivering 250 N grip force on a 40 mm object with a surface pressure limit of 1 MPa; a gear pair transmitting 30 N·m at a stated ratio. The design variables are the module allocation per tool, the geometry within each tool, and the connector layout.

## Solver and engine

- Kinematics and statics: `pinneapple_simulation.multibody.arm` and `tree` for the linkage and the wrench's load path; `pinneapple_design.bricks.Assembly` and `analyse_assembly` for the joint-force solution of the assembled module frame, which is exactly the solver this problem needs.
- Member strength: `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.beam_fe` for the handle and the linkage members, `solid_fem` for the connector detail, `plane_stress_fem` for the gear tooth section.
- Contact and friction: a stated Coulomb model with the jaw and tooth contact pressures computed from the force and the contact area; `pinneapple_simulation.particle_dynamics.terramechanics` contact conventions are not applicable and the model is written in the experiment module.
- Layout optimisation: `pinneapple_design.topology` for the member layout within a tool, with the mass constraint enforced.
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A connector model with stiffness and capacity** for the general module set: tensile, shear and moment capacity with a compliance, and a failure criterion, as a reusable component. `bricks` handles interlocking contact between identical blocks and does not carry a strength-rated general connector.
2. **A per-tool load-path model**: three modules (`tools/wrench.py`, `tools/gripper.py`, `tools/gear.py`) each with a closed-form preliminary sizing and a finite-element check, so the tool's limit is computed and not assumed.
3. **A gear tooth model**: the Lewis bending and the Hertzian contact expressions implemented with the tooth geometry derived from the module ring, plus the module-count constraint (the teeth must be modules).
4. **A mass-allocation optimisation across the three tools**: the deliverable is the Pareto surface over the three function scores at a fixed total mass, so the trade is visible rather than a single design.
5. **A failure-attribution report**: for each tool, which component fails at the required load — the member, the connector, the contact, or the tooth — with the margin. This is the result the issue exists to produce.
6. **A baseline comparison** against three single-purpose tools of the same total mass, built conventionally, so the modular penalty is a measured number.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify each load-path model against a closed form: the cantilever handle against `sigma = Mc/I`, the linkage against the closed-form transmission ratio with a stated efficiency, the gear tooth against the standard Lewis and Hertzian values for a steel gear of the same module.
2. Verify the connector model against the `bricks` analysis on a shared test case, where both apply, and report the difference.
3. Single-tool baselines: the best modular wrench, gripper and gear when each gets the whole 60-module budget, which is the upper bound per function.
4. Mass-split study: allocate the module budget in steps of 10 percent between the three tools, 100 allocations per pair, and score all three functions at each allocation.
5. Pareto analysis over the three function scores, with the binding constraint per point.
6. Failure attribution at the required loads for the best allocation.
7. Conventional baselines: three single-purpose tools at the same total mass, scored on the same metrics.
8. Compute budget: seconds per statics solve, thousands of solves — order 10^1 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The three closed-form expressions — cantilever bending, linkage transmission ratio, Lewis and Hertzian tooth stress — are the model references, and each model must reproduce its expression within a stated tolerance before it is used for the trade.
- The `bricks` cross-check on the connector model is the internal consistency test.
- The single-tool optima at the full budget are the per-function upper bounds: no allocation can exceed them, and the gap between the bound and the allocation result is the cost of universality.
- The three conventional single-purpose tools at equal mass are the engineering baseline: if the modular set does not beat them on any function, the report must say that plainly.
- Global equilibrium at every joint of every assembly, with the residual reported, since a statics result without equilibrium is meaningless.

## Metrics

- Transmitted torque `T`, N·m, for the wrench, and its fraction of the requirement.
- Grip force `F_grip`, N, and peak contact pressure at the jaw, MPa, against the object's limit.
- Tangential tooth load and the resulting bending and contact stresses, MPa, against the stated allowables.
- Function score per tool: the achieved value divided by the requirement, capped at one, dimensionless.
- Binding constraint per tool, as a label: member, connector, contact or tooth.
- Modular penalty per tool: the achieved function divided by the conventional single-purpose tool's value, dimensionless, at equal total mass.

## Deliverables

- The connector model and the three tool modules as importable code with the closed-form verification tests.
- The Pareto surface over the three function scores, as data and figure, with the binding constraint labelled per region.
- The failure-attribution table for each tool at the required load.
- The comparison table against the conventional tools at equal mass.
- Assembly renders of the three tools from the same module inventory, with the module count per tool annotated — the figure that shows what the trade actually costs.
- A `pinneapple_lab` manifest per allocation, with the module count and the connector layout.
- A report whose first paragraph names the tool that fails first and the margin by which it fails.

## Acceptance criteria

- Each load-path model reproduces its closed-form reference within 3 percent on the verification case.
- The global equilibrium residual is below 1e-9 relative for every reported assembly.
- Every function score is accompanied by its binding constraint; no score is reported without the failure attribution.
- The Pareto surface covers at least three points that are non-dominated, and the report identifies the allocation at which two constraints bind simultaneously.
- The modular penalty is reported for all three tools, and any tool on which the modular set loses to the conventional tool is stated in the summary.
- The mass budget is verified exactly: the module count across the three tools equals the inventory, reported per allocation.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for the statics and the mechanism analysis; `extrapolated` for a modular inventory of the stated connector capacity, since the connector is the binding element and its strength and stiffness are assumed; `hypothetical` for a single set that performs all three functions to engineering standard, which the trade is designed to test rather than assume. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (closed-form references, equilibrium, mass budget), `numerical_stability` (mesh convergence on the tooth and connector, optimisation repeatability), `physical_plausibility` (the connector capacities against real small-connector values, the contact pressures against real object limits), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (module count, connector capacity). `experimental_validation` is not assessed: nothing is built or tested. `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the mass and strength budgets.

To move up one class, in order: (1) a measured connector strength and stiffness, including the backlash and the repeatability of the connection, since a real modular tool's accuracy is set by the connector and not by the module; (2) a measured assembly time and error rate, since reconfiguration cost is half of what "universal" means in practice; (3) a hardware demonstration of one function at the required load; (4) a wear and fatigue study, since a connector loaded repeatedly at its capacity is a different component from one loaded once. Item 1 is the deciding assumption and it is the one most easily measured, so the experiment is worth running before the hardware work.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The connector is given a strength the real connector does not have, and the result is optimistic.** Mitigation: the capacity is stated as a range and the sensitivity of each function score to it is reported; the report gives the capacity at which the conclusion reverses.
2. **The gear's requirement is met by ignoring the module-count constraint** — teeth that are not modules. Mitigation: the tooth geometry is derived from the module ring, and the constraint is enforced in the model and verified in the output.
3. **The conventional baseline is a straw man.** Mitigation: it is built at the same total mass with stated material and geometry, and its sizing follows the same closed forms.
4. **The trade study is reported as a single best allocation.** Mitigation: the Pareto surface with per-region binding constraints is the deliverable, and a single point fails the issue.
5. **Contact pressure is computed from force over a nominal area and is not a real pressure.** Mitigation: the contact model is stated, the Hertzian form is used where the geometry allows it, and the pressure metric is reported with its model.

## Out of scope

- Tool dynamics, impact loading and vibration during use.
- Assembly time, reconfiguration sequence and the mechanism that performs it — those belong to D1 and D24.
- Wear, fatigue and the durability of the connector under repeated loading.
- Any human-factors or safety claim about the tools in use.
