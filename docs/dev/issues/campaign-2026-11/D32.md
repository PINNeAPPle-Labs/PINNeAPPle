## Summary

Four real trapping mechanisms — magnetic, acoustic, optical and electrostatic — are computed for objects of stated size, and the experiment produces one table: which mechanism, at which object size, gives how many newtons of trapping force and how much stiffness, and how much energy it deposits in the object. There is no fictional mechanism in the list; the point is that "moving an object without touching it" is a solved problem at small scale and a scaling problem at large scale, and the table is the scaling law made concrete.

## Question

For each of the four mechanisms, what trapping force and stiffness are achievable for an object of stated size and material at a stated input power or field, and over what size range does each mechanism remain the best choice?

## Why run it

- The scaling laws are the whole content and they are brutal: optical gradient force scales as `a^3` and the weight as `a^3`, but the power needed scales differently, so the crossover is a number rather than an intuition.
- The table is directly reusable: it tells a designer which mechanism to reach for at a given object size, and where each one stops working, with the reason named.
- Every entry has a closed-form expression, so the experiment is a calculation with a verification path, which is the cheapest kind of useful result.

## Physical model

Each mechanism is computed from its own expression, at the same stated conditions where the comparison is meaningful, and the comparison table reports the conditions because they are not equivalent.

**Magnetic.** For a superparamagnetic bead of radius `a` with volume susceptibility `chi` in a field `B`:

    F = (2 pi a^3 chi / mu_0) grad(B^2)   (point-dipole approximation, valid for a << field length scale)

with the stiffness `k = dF/dx` from the second derivative of `B^2`. A permanent magnet and a coil pair provide the field; the achievable gradient at a stated distance is the input.

**Acoustic.** The Gor'kov potential for a small sphere in a standing wave:

    U = 2 pi a^3 [ f_1 <p^2>/(3 rho_0 c^2) - f_2 (3 rho_0/4) <v^2> ]
    F = -grad(U),  which near a node gives k ~ 2 pi a^3 f_1 k_w^2 E_ac / (3 ...)

with `f_1`, `f_2` the monopole and dipole contrast factors. The input is the acoustic energy density, and the constraint is the heating at the stated intensity.

**Optical.** Radiation pressure and the gradient force for a Rayleigh particle:

    F_grad = (2 pi a^3 / c) ( (m^2-1)/(m^2+2) ) grad(I)
    F_scatter = (128 pi^5 a^6 / (3 lambda^4 c)) ( (m^2-1)/(m^2+2) )^2 I

with the wavelength, the numerical aperture, and the trapping condition that the gradient force exceeds the scattering force and the Brownian force.

**Electrostatic.** A charged or polarizable particle in a quadrupole field, `F = q E` or `F = 2 pi a^3 eps_0 ((eps_p - eps_m)/(eps_p + 2 eps_m)) grad(E^2)`, with the constraint that the charge is screened at the stated ionic strength, which is what limits the mechanism in any conductive medium.

The comparison is made at a stated common budget: for a magnetic and an electrostatic trap, a stated field at the object; for acoustic and optical, a stated input power and the resulting intensity at the object. The dimensionless group that decides which wins is not one number but the ratio of the force to the object's weight and to the Brownian force at room temperature, and both ratios are reported.

Validity and omission: point-particle approximations with the stated size limits, no hydrodynamic interactions (which matter for a particle near a wall), no optical binding between multiple particles, no photophoretic or thermal effects, and no electro-osmotic flow. The model deliberately omits the apparatus: a real optical trap includes a microscope, a real acoustic trap includes a transducer and a cavity, and neither is designed here.

## Geometry and inputs

Objects: spheres of radius 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, 50 and 100 µm, with stated densities and refractive indices and either a magnetic susceptibility, an acoustic contrast factor or a dielectric constant as appropriate. Field and power inputs: a magnetic gradient from `10^-1` to `10^3` T/m, an acoustic intensity of 1 W/cm² to 1 kW/cm² at 1 MHz, an optical power of 1 mW to 10 W at 1064 nm with a numerical aperture of 0.9, and an electrostatic field of `10^3` to `10^6` V/m at a stated ionic strength of 10^-3 and 10^-1 mol/L.

## Solver and engine

- Force expressions and stiffness: implemented directly from the closed forms in the experiment module, with the symbolic derivative used for the stiffness where the expression is analytic (`pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.symbolic_fd` for the verification of the analytic derivative).
- Field solutions for the non-ideal cases: `pinneapple_simulation.electromagnetics.statics` and `fdtd` for the field and its gradient from a real coil or magnet arrangement, and `electromagnetics.waves`, `mie` and `media` for the optical case including the Mie regime where the Rayleigh approximation fails.
- Acoustic field: `pinneapple_analysis.inspection.phased_array_ultrasonic` for field synthesis, and `electromagnetics.waves` for the analogous wave formulism where the standing-wave structure matters.
- Fluid and Brownian: `pinneapple_simulation.numerical_solvers.sph`/`isph` where a hydrodynamic check is wanted, and the fluctuation-dissipation relation for the Brownian force.
- Verification: `pp.closed_form` for the order-of-magnitude check; `pp.compare` between the closed form and the Mie solution; `pinneapple_analysis.verification.convergence`.
- Recording: `pinneapple_lab.experiment`.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **A trap-force and stiffness calculator per mechanism**: `pinneapple_physics/trap.py` with `magnetic_trap(...)`, `acoustic_trap(...)`, `optical_trap(...)` and `electrostatic_trap(...)`, each returning force, stiffness, the input budget consumed and the validity flag for its point-particle approximation. No trap-stiffness calculation exists in the library.
2. **A validity checker per mechanism**: the size-to-wavelength ratio for the acoustic and optical cases (so the Rayleigh approximation is flagged outside its range and the Mie path is used), the size-to-field-scale ratio for the magnetic case, and the Debye length for the electrostatic case. Without this the table would report a point-particle number outside its range.
3. **A common comparison framework**: the ratio of trap force to weight and to the Brownian force at room temperature for each mechanism and size, computed and reported for every row, since force in newtons alone is not comparable across objects of different mass.
4. **A Mie and acoustic-scattering path** for the sizes where the Rayleigh approximation fails, using the existing `mie` module, so the size range is covered honestly rather than truncated at the approximation's limit.
5. **A heating and energy-deposition calculation**: the energy deposited in the object per unit time for each mechanism at its stated input, reported alongside the force, since that is the quantity that decides whether the mechanism damages what it holds.
6. **The comparison table generator** with the conditions recorded per row, so no row can be read without the input it required.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify each closed form against a published measurement or an independent implementation: the optical gradient force against a measured trap stiffness, the magnetic force against a known bead measurement, the acoustic force against a published Gor'kov result. Where a measurement is not available, state it and use the Mie path as the cross-check.
2. Verify the derivatives: the stiffness must match the numerical derivative of the force to 1e-6 relative everywhere the closed form is used.
3. Table generation: 8 sizes by 4 mechanisms by 2 input levels (low and high within each mechanism's stated range) = 64 rows, each with force, stiffness, weight ratio, Brownian ratio, energy deposition and validity flag.
4. Size-range determination: for each mechanism, the contiguous size range in which it produces a force above the Brownian force at room temperature with the high input; report the endpoints.
5. Best-mechanism map: for each size, which mechanism gives the largest force per unit input, with the input units stated, and the crossover sizes.
6. Real-field checks: one magnetic and one optical case computed with the full field solution rather than the closed form, so the point approximation's error is a reported number.
7. Compute budget: the closed forms are instant; the field and Mie solutions are seconds to minutes — order 10^0 CPU-hours.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The closed forms are the primary reference and each is verified against the numerical derivative (for stiffness) and against a published measurement or the Mie path (for the force).
- The Mie solution is the reference for the optical case above the Rayleigh limit, and the discrepancy at the boundary is a reported check on the crossover between the two.
- The Brownian force at room temperature is the physical baseline every trap must exceed for it to hold anything at all; a row whose force is below it is reported as a non-trap.
- Weight is the second baseline: the ratio of force to weight is what decides whether the mechanism could ever move a macroscopic object, and it is the number that ends the discussion.
- `pp.closed_form` is used for an order-of-magnitude consistency check on the optical and radiation-pressure entries.

## Metrics

- Trapping force `F_trap`, N, at the stated input, with the input stated in the row.
- Trap stiffness `k`, N/m, from the force gradient at the trap centre.
- Force-to-weight ratio, dimensionless, and force-to-Brownian-force ratio, dimensionless.
- Energy deposition in the object, W, and the corresponding temperature-rise estimate for the stated object and medium.
- Validity flag and the approximation used, per row.
- Crossover size between mechanisms, in µm, from the best-mechanism map.
- Input budget consumed, in the mechanism's own units, so no row hides an unphysical input.

## Deliverables

- The trap calculator with the four mechanisms, the validity checker and the comparison-table generator as importable code with the derivative and Mie verification tests.
- The 64-row table, as CSV and as a rendered table, with every row carrying its input and validity flag.
- The size-range and best-mechanism maps as figures, one log-log plot per mechanism of force against size, with the Brownian and weight lines drawn.
- The real-field comparison for one magnetic and one optical case.
- A figure of the force-to-weight ratio against object size across the four mechanisms, which is the single image that carries the argument.
- A `pinneapple_lab` manifest per row group.
- A report whose first paragraph gives the crossover size above which no mechanism in the table applies.

## Acceptance criteria

- Every closed-form force reproduces the numerical derivative of its potential to 1e-6 relative where the potential form exists, and each stiffness matches the numerical derivative of the force to 1e-6.
- The optical path switches to the Mie solution above the stated size limit, and the two agree at the crossover within 10 percent.
- Every table row states the input it required and the validity flag of its approximation; a row without either fails the issue.
- Every row reports the force-to-weight and force-to-Brownian ratios, so no entry is a bare force in newtons.
- The report identifies the object size above which none of the four mechanisms produces a force above the Brownian force at the stated maximum inputs.
- The first paragraph of the report contains the newtons-per-watt figure for the optical case and the newtons-per-tesla-squared-per-metre figure for the magnetic case, so the reader has the scaling immediately.
- Any mechanism whose best case requires an input outside a physically achievable range is labelled as such rather than reported as the winner.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established` for all four mechanisms at small scale, each demonstrated experimentally and in commercial instruments for optical and magnetic tweezers; `extrapolated` at the upper size bounds, where the required input grows faster than the force; `fictional` for holding a macroscopic rigid object, which the force-to-weight ratio excludes by many orders of magnitude rather than by argument. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (derivative checks, potential forms, Mie crossover), `numerical_stability` (field-solution convergence, approximation boundary), `physical_plausibility` (published trap stiffnesses for the optical and magnetic cases, the actual range of achievable gradients and intensities), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (input range, object properties). `experimental_validation` is not performed here, although the mechanisms themselves are experimentally established and the report should say so rather than imply a gap. `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the input budgets.

There is no class to move up for the small-scale mechanisms. The honest limitation is the one the table shows: the force scales with the object's volume for the gradient mechanisms and the weight scales with the mass, so the ratio is scale-invariant only while the achievable gradient is, and the achievable gradient falls with apparatus size. The report must state that the table's upper rows are limited by the apparatus and not by the physics.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **A point-particle expression is used outside its range and the table claims a force that does not exist.** Mitigation: the validity flag and the Mie path are per-row requirements, and the crossover is checked.
2. **The input conditions are not comparable across mechanisms and the "best" column is meaningless.** Mitigation: the inputs are stated per row in the mechanism's own units, and the best-mechanism map is presented as a function of the input available rather than as an absolute ranking.
3. **Energy deposition is neglected and a trap that would damage its object looks good.** Mitigation: energy deposition and a temperature-rise estimate are required columns.
4. **The magnetic force is computed from a gradient that no apparatus can produce.** Mitigation: the achievable gradient range is stated with its source, and rows outside it are labelled.
5. **A single number is quoted from the table without its row conditions.** Mitigation: the table's rows carry their conditions and the report's headline numbers state theirs inline.

## Out of scope

- Apparatus design, optical systems, transducers and coil winding.
- Trapping in a living organism and any biomedical application.
- Multi-particle effects: optical binding, acoustic streaming between particles, collective trapping.
- Hydrodynamic interactions with walls and with other particles beyond the stated single-particle model.
