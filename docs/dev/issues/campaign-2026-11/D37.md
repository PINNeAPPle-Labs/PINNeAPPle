## Summary

For a system that has a physics model and an expensive solver, a decision aid enumerates the candidate actions, propagates each through a surrogate with its uncertainty, ranks them, and then verifies the chosen action with the full solver. The scientific content is not the ranking but its reliability: did the ranking survive verification, and was the uncertainty that drove the ranking calibrated? The output is the regret over a held-out decision set — how much worse the chosen action turned out to be, compared with the best action a brute-force search would have found — and the compute cost that was saved to achieve it.

## Question

Over a held-out set of decision problems, what is the regret of the action chosen via the surrogate against the brute-force best action, and is the surrogate's predictive uncertainty calibrated across the action space?

## Why run it

- The claim that a surrogate can guide decisions is only meaningful with a regret number and a calibration check, and both are cheap to produce with the machinery that already exists.
- It converts an unquantified engineering practice — using a cheap model to prune a design space — into a measurable one, with the cost saving reported alongside the regret, so the trade is explicit.
- The failure mode, which is that the surrogate ranks correctly in the region it was trained on and arbitrarily outside it, is exactly the behaviour a held-out decision set exposes.

## Physical model

The system is a parameterised physics model with a response `y = f(x)` computed by an expensive solver, an action `a` that selects a point or a policy in the parameter space, and an objective `J(a)` that is a functional of the response. The decision problem is `a* = argmax_a J(a)` over a candidate set, and the protocol answers it two ways.

**Brute force.** Evaluate `J` with the full solver at every candidate action, which gives `J_true(a)` for all `a` and therefore the true best action and the true best value.

**Surrogate-guided.** Fit a surrogate to a training sample of the full solver, predict with uncertainty, rank the candidates by an acquisition rule, verify only the top few with the full solver, and take the best verified one.

Two quantities follow directly. The regret is `J_true(a_best) - J_true(a_chosen)`, normalised by the spread of `J_true` over the candidate set so it is comparable across problems. The calibration is measured by the reliability of the predictive intervals: the fraction of held-out points falling inside the stated interval against the nominal level, and the sharpness of those intervals. A surrogate whose intervals are too narrow will rank confidently and be wrong, which is the failure this experiment is designed to detect.

The dimensionless quantities: the sample ratio (training points over candidate points), the objective's dynamic range relative to the surrogate's predictive spread, and the normalised regret. Validity and omission: this is a statistical protocol over a deterministic solver, so the only randomness is the sampling and the surrogate's own stochasticity, both seeded. It omits the case where the action space is continuous and the brute-force answer is itself approximate — the candidate set is finite and enumerated, precisely so the reference is exact and the regret is not confounded.

## Geometry and inputs

Test systems: (a) the pipe-bend and heated-pipe pipelines from the simulation package, where the action is a geometry and flow choice and the objective is a pressure-drop or heat-transfer figure of merit; (b) a heat-sink configuration from the thermal design package, where the action is a fin layout; (c) a surrogate-defined synthetic objective with a known landscape, which is the control case where the correct answer is known exactly. Candidate sets of 400 to 2000 actions per problem. Training samples from 20 to 400 points. Held-out decision set: 30 decision problems per system, fixed before the run.

## What is missing (the work)

1. **An action-enumeration layer**: `pinneapple_decision/actions.py` with `enumerate_actions(space, resolution)` producing a discrete candidate set with its coverage properties reported, and `objective(action, response)` computing the figure of merit. Enumeration over a design space is implicit in `Sweep` and does not exist as a first-class, reportable object.
2. **A regret protocol**: `run_decision_problem(problem, surrogate, n_train, seed)` that runs the brute force, the surrogate-guided choice and the verification, and returns the true best, the chosen action, the regret and the verification cost saved. Nothing in the library computes a regret.
3. **A calibration check**: reliability diagrams and interval coverage against the nominal level, plus a sharpness measure, over the held-out decision set. `trust_report` summarises trust for one surrogate; the coverage measurement across a decision set does not exist.
4. **An acquisition-rule comparison**: at least three rules (greedy predicted value, predicted value plus an exploration bonus, and a probability-of-improvement rule), run on the same problems, so the regret is attributed to the rule and not only to the surrogate.
5. **A cost accounting**: full-solver evaluations consumed by each path, and wall time, so the saved cost is a measured number and not an estimate from a formula.
6. **A decision-set generator with a fixed seed and a stored manifest**, since the held-out property is only meaningful if the set is frozen before the run.

## Experiment protocol

1. Verify the pipeline and thermal cases against their own trust paths: `heatsink.trust` and `trust_report` on a known configuration, and one `run_pipeline` case against its documented reference.
2. Brute-force reference: for each of the 30 decision problems per system, evaluate the full solver at every candidate action. This is the expensive part and it is unavoidable; the cost is reported.
3. Surrogate-guided: for each problem, training sample sizes of 20, 50, 100, 200 and 400, three seeds, three acquisition rules, verifying the top 3 candidates. This yields 30 by 3 problems by 5 sample sizes by 3 rules by 3 seeds = 4050 guided runs, most of them cheap.
4. Regret computation: normalised regret per problem, aggregated as a median with the interquartile range, per sample size and per rule.
5. Calibration: interval coverage and sharpness per sample size, on the held-out points.
6. Cost accounting: full-solver evaluations consumed, and wall time, per path.
7. The synthetic control: the same protocol on the known landscape, where the regret must be zero at the largest sample size; a nonzero result there indicates a protocol bug.
8. Compute budget: the brute force dominates — order 10^3 full-solver runs for the physical systems, so order 10^2 to 10^3 CPU-hours, with the guided paths negligible.

## Baselines, references and verification

- The brute-force result is the exact reference for the physical systems, since the candidate set is finite and fully evaluated. This is what makes the regret exact rather than approximate.
- The synthetic control with a known landscape is the protocol's correctness check: the guided path must find the true optimum at large sample size, and any failure is a bug, not a finding.
- A random-choice baseline and a greedy-prediction baseline bracket the acquisition rules: a rule that does not beat random is not doing anything, and the greedy baseline shows whether the exploration term earns its cost.
- The calibration reference is the nominal coverage level; a surrogate that is over-confident is detectable as coverage below nominal, which is the specific failure the protocol is designed to catch.
- `pinneapple_analysis.cost.equal_budget` provides the standard accounting so the cost comparison is consistent with the library's benchmark conventions.

## Metrics

- Normalised regret `R`, dimensionless, the objective gap divided by the dynamic range of the true objective over the candidate set, per problem, reported as a median and interquartile range.
- Worst-case regret, the maximum over the held-out decision set, which is the number a practitioner cares about.
- Verification success rate: the fraction of problems where the verified best candidate equals the true best, percent.
- Interval coverage at the 90 percent nominal level, percent, and mean interval width as the sharpness measure.
- Cost ratio: full-solver evaluations in the guided path divided by the brute-force count, dimensionless, and the wall-time ratio.
- Regret per unit cost saved, which is the single trade-off figure the report leads with.

## Deliverables

- The action-enumeration layer, the regret protocol and the calibration check as importable code with the synthetic-control test.
- The regret and calibration tables per system, sample size, rule and seed, as CSV.
- The reliability diagrams and the regret-against-sample-size curves, as figures.
- The cost-saving table with the evaluations consumed and the wall time per path.
- A worked example per physical system: the candidate set, the true best, the chosen action, and the regret, so the reader can see a single decision in full.
- A `pinneapple_lab` manifest per guided run, with the seed, the sample size and the rule.
- A report whose first paragraph gives the median regret and the cost ratio at the recommended sample size.

## Acceptance criteria

- The synthetic control reaches zero regret at the largest sample size for all seeds; a nonzero result stops the experiment as a protocol defect.
- Every regret is computed against a fully evaluated candidate set, and the evaluation count for the brute force is reported per problem.
- Interval coverage is reported against the nominal level for every sample size, and any case with coverage below nominal by more than a stated margin is reported as a calibration failure.
- The acquisition rules are compared on the same problems and seeds, and the report states which rule wins and by how much.
- The cost ratio is measured, not estimated, and the wall time is recorded alongside the evaluation count.
- The worst-case regret is reported alongside the median, and no result is reported from the median alone.
- Every reported number is accompanied by the sample size and the number of seeds behind it.

## Realizability and evidence class

`PLAUSIBILITY_CLASS`: `established`. Surrogate-assisted optimisation and its calibration are standard practice, the protocol's reference is exact, and the experiment's only novelty is that it reports the regret and the calibration instead of the speed-up alone. Axes this run fills: `mathematical_consistency` (synthetic control, exact brute-force reference, calibration against the nominal level), `numerical_stability` (seed repeatability, sample-size dependence), `physical_plausibility` (the physical systems are the library's own verified cases), `uncertainty_sensitivity` (the calibration and sharpness measurements, which are the point). `experimental_validation` is not applicable. `technological_feasibility` is partly addressed by the cost ratio.

There is no class to move up. The limitation is one of scope and is worth stating: the candidate set is discrete and finite, which makes the reference exact but also makes the problem easier than a continuous design space; and the surrogate is fit to samples of a deterministic solver, so there is no measurement noise and the calibration result would differ with noisy data. The report must state both, and the honest transfer claim is only to problems with the same structure: a deterministic expensive solver, a finite candidate set and a low-dimensional action space.

## Risks and failure modes

1. **The held-out decision set leaks into the surrogate's training or into the choice of the acquisition rule.** Mitigation: the set and its manifest are frozen before any guided run, and the hash is recorded.
2. **The brute-force reference is itself incomplete, so the regret is measured against the wrong answer.** Mitigation: the candidate set is fully evaluated and the evaluation count is compared with the candidate count, with a mismatch being a defect.
3. **The regret is averaged into a mean and hides a catastrophic case.** Mitigation: the worst-case regret over the held-out set is a required metric.
4. **The calibration is measured on the training points.** Mitigation: coverage is computed on held-out points only, and the split is part of the frozen manifest.
5. **The cost saving is quoted from a formula rather than measured.** Mitigation: evaluations and wall time are recorded per path, and the metric is the measured ratio.

## Out of scope

- Continuous action spaces and the optimisation of the acquisition rule itself.
- Multi-fidelity surrogate hierarchies and the allocation of a budget across fidelities.
- Human decision-making, interfaces and any claim about decision support for operators.
- Any domain-specific recommendation about the systems used as test cases.
