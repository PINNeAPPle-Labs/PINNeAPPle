    Detect false data injection with physics residuals¶

    An attacker who rewrites a sensor stream in a plant keeps every value plausible on its own and close to its own history. That is exactly what a purely data-driven detector learns, so it is what such a detector checks. What the attacker cannot keep without a model of the plant is the physics that ties several sensors together: a tank level must integrate the measured in- and outflows, a valve passes q = sqrt(a + b * dh) for the head across it.

    pinneapple_security.cps turns those laws into residual channels, identifies their constants from trusted data (a grey-box twin: no hand-tuned coefficients), and raises alarms with a per-channel CUSUM calibrated to a false-alarm budget. It is a defensive tool: it reads sensor data and reports which balance broke.

    The three-tank example¶

    reservoir --pump Fp--> T1 --valve F12--> T2 --valve F23--> T3 --demand Fd--> consumers

    from pinneapple_security.cps import FlowLaw, MassBalance, PhysicsResidualDetector, episode_metrics
from pinneapple_security.cps_bench import inject, simulate_three_tank

train = simulate_three_tank(7, seed=0)            # dict of sensor columns, one sample per minute
calib = simulate_three_tank(7, seed=1)            # clean data the detector does not fit on
test = simulate_three_tank(10, seed=2)

det = PhysicsResidualDetector(components=[
    MassBalance("h1", ["Fp"], ["F12"], dt=60, window=1),   # area identified by least squares
    MassBalance("h2", ["F12"], ["F23"], dt=60, window=1),
    MassBalance("h3", ["F23"], ["Fd"], dt=60, window=1),
    FlowLaw("F12", "h1", "h2", midpoint=True),             # q^2 = a + b (h1 - h2), a and b identified
    FlowLaw("F23", "h2", "h3", midpoint=True),
]).fit(train)
det.calibrate(calib, false_alarms_per_day=1.0)

attacked = inject(test, "bias", [(3000, 3240)])            # F12 meter reads 4 % high for four hours
out = det.detect(attacked)
out["first_alarm"], out["channels"][:3]                    # time of the first alarm, balances blamed
episode_metrics(out["alarms"], [(3000, 3240)], len(test["h1"]))

    Your own plant needs only the column names and the laws that hold between them. Any object with name, fit(D) and residual(D) -> array is a component, so an energy balance of a heat exchanger, a pump curve or a state-estimation residual of a power network plugs in the same way.

    Choices that matter¶

    • window=1 for storage balances when the flow meters report averages over the sample interval: the residual is then white noise, which is what the CUSUM assumes. Longer windows reduce level noise but make the residual autocorrelated, and the calibrated threshold grows.
    • midpoint=True for flow laws with averaged flows and levels read at the start of the interval. Without it the law is evaluated at the wrong head and the residual carries a bias that changes with the operating point.
    • Calibrate on clean data the detector was not fitted on, at least as long as the attack episodes you care about. calibrate bisects the threshold to meet the budget exactly on that trace.

    Benchmark: physics against data-driven detectors¶

    python examples/security/02_physics_residual_fdi.py --seeds 0 1 2 fits four detectors on a trusted week, calibrates each to one false alarm per day on another clean week, then injects six attacks in 20 four-hour episodes of a ten-day test trace. The same CUSUM alarm rule is used for every detector, so the comparison measures the residuals, not the alarm logic. Baselines: a linear autoregressive twin of all sensors (three lags, ridge), PCA of sensors and increments (Q statistic), and an isolation forest.

    Mean of three seeds. AUC is episode-level (largest statistic in each attack episode against every clean window of the same length); "det" is the fraction of episodes detected, "delay" the median delay in minutes, "loc" the fraction of detections whose blamed channel contains the attacked sensor.

    What the numbers say:

    • In the regime it was trained on, a good data-driven twin (the AR model) detects as well as the physics: both reach AUC 1.0. Physics is faster on the bias (8 vs 16 min) and slower on the slow level ramp (108 vs 82 min: a level drift barely moves a storage balance until the valve laws see it), and it points at the right sensor far more often (0.98 vs 0.03 for the bias, 0.90 vs 0.07 for metering fraud).
    • When the plant moves to an operating point the data never covered, the data-driven detectors flood the operator (84 and 32 false alarms per day) while the physics residuals stay calibrated (0.47 per day) and keep AUC 1.0. This is the practical argument for physics: plants change set-points, seasons and demand far more often than anyone refits a detector.
    • The isolation forest and PCA, which only look at the joint distribution of the readings, miss most attacks that keep values in range.

    Limits¶

    • Coordinated replay of every sensor is physically consistent. Both residual detectors catch it here only because the splice from the live to the replayed recording is a discontinuity (delay 0). An attacker who aligns the splice, or one who knows the plant model and injects consistent data (a stealthy attack in the sense of Liu, Ning and Reiter 2009), is invisible to any residual test. Pair the detector with anomaly.replay_score, with signed data paths (pinneapple_security.signing) and with physical watermarking where the control loop allows it.
    • The benchmark is simulated (seeded, reproducible). Public ICS datasets either need a signed request (SWaT, WADI) or were not reachable when this was written; an adapter for BATADAL is on the roadmap.
    • Attacks rewrite the reported readings only; the controller here acts on the true T1 level, so no attack changes the physical trajectory.
